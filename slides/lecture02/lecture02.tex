\section{Star formation and protoplanetary disks}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ── Molecular clouds ────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Every planetary system starts in a cold cloud}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    Molecular clouds: $T \sim 10$--$20$ K, mostly $\mathrm{H_2}$ and helium, with $\sim 1\%$ by mass in microscopic \textbf{dust grains}.
    \vskip6pt\relax
    Collapse begins when self-gravity beats thermal and magnetic pressure support. The critical mass is the \textbf{Jeans mass}:
    \[
      M_J \sim \frac{c_s^3}{\sqrt{G^3 \rho}}
    \]

    \column{0.44\textwidth}
    \begin{block}{Order of magnitude}
      For $T \approx 10$ K and $n_{\mathrm{H_2}} \approx 10^4$ cm$^{-3}$:
      \[
        M_J \sim 1\text{--}10\ \Msun
      \]
    \end{block}
    \vskip8pt\relax
    Not a coincidence: the Jeans mass under molecular-cloud conditions is \emph{comparable to a stellar mass}. Clouds fragment into star-sized pieces.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ── Carina cliffs ───────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Star formation, caught in the act}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth, height=0.72\textheight, keepaspectratio]{carina_cliffs_jwst}
    \source{NASA/ESA/CSA/STScI (JWST/NIRCam)}

    \column{0.42\textwidth}
    The ``Cosmic Cliffs'' at the edge of the Carina Nebula.
    \vskip6pt\relax
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Embedded protostars and outflows along the eroding cloud edge.
      \item Ultraviolet radiation from young massive stars sculpts the gas.
      \item A present-day analogue of the environment the Solar System was born in.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ── Angular momentum ────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Why collapse makes a disk, not a ball}
  Angular momentum is the decisive constraint: even a slow initial rotation is amplified enormously by contraction. For a cloud core of radius $R_0 \sim 0.1$ pc collapsing to stellar scales ($\sim 1\,\Rsun$):
  \[
    \frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{final}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{initial}}} \;=\; \left(\frac{R_0}{\Rsun}\right)^{\!2} \;\sim\; 10^{13}
  \]
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item No star could sustain that spin: the material cannot all fall onto the protostar.
    \item Instead it settles into a rotating, flattened \textbf{protoplanetary disk}.
    \item The protostar accretes from it over $\sim 1$--$3$ Myr; the disk becomes the raw material for planets.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \keyresult{Planet formation is a by-product of the angular-momentum problem of star formation.}
\end{frame}

% ── HH 30 ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{A disk seen edge-on: HH 30}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth, height=0.72\textheight, keepaspectratio]{hh30_edge_on}
    \source{NASA/ESA/STScI (HST)}

    \column{0.49\textwidth}
    The geometry is directly visible:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Dark lane}: the dusty midplane, opaque even in the optical.
      \item \textbf{Two bright lobes}: starlight scattering off the upper and lower disk surfaces.
      \item The star itself is hidden behind the midplane.
    \end{itemize}
    \vskip6pt\relax
    This is what ``geometrically thin and flared'' looks like on the sky.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ── Disk schematic hero ─────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \centering
  \makebox[\linewidth][c]{\includegraphics[width=0.95\paperwidth, height=0.85\paperheight, keepaspectratio]{andrews2020_disk_schematic}}\\
  {\footnotesize Schematic cross-section of a protoplanetary disk. Adapted from Andrews (2020), Fig.~1.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the disk schematic}
  Gas in grayscale; solids drawn with \emph{exaggerated} sizes.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Left half, what we observe}: emission lines ($\mathrm{CO}$ and others) from the warm surface; infrared scattered light from small grains in the atmosphere; (sub-)mm/cm continuum from large grains near the midplane.
    \item \textbf{Right half, the vocabulary}: a gas \emph{atmosphere} holding small suspended grains, and a \emph{midplane} layer of gas plus larger solids.
    \item \textbf{Flaring}: the disk gets vertically thicker, in relative terms, with distance.
    \item \textbf{Settling}: bigger particles sink toward the midplane, so different wavelengths trace different heights \emph{and} different particle sizes.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \keyresult{Every observation of a disk is a slice through this structure: know which slice before interpreting it.}
\end{frame}

% ── Disk properties ─────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Disk structure in four numbers}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Temperature gradient}: $> 1500$ K in the innermost disk, where silicates vaporise, falling to $< 30$ K in the outer disk. This profile decides what can condense where.
    \item \textbf{Surface density}: $\Sigma(r) \propto r^{-p}$ with $p \approx 0.5$--$1.5$. Densest in the inner disk, but the \emph{mass budget} is dominated by the far larger area outside.
    \item \textbf{Disk mass}: from a few percent of the stellar mass in the youngest embedded systems, down to $\sim 0.1\%$ by the Class II stage. These estimates carry large systematic uncertainty.
    \item \textbf{Dust-to-gas ratio}: $\sim 1\%$ inherited from the interstellar medium, but locally enhanced by settling and radial drift, which is what makes planet formation possible.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  Vertical structure: scale height $H/r \sim 0.03$--$0.1$, increasing outward (a \textbf{flared} disk).
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Andrews (2020); Miotello et al.\ (2023).}
\end{frame}

% ── Snow line ───────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The snow line}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    The distance beyond which water condenses as ice. In the solar nebula: roughly \textbf{2.5--3.5 AU}, between Mars and Jupiter.
    \vskip6pt\relax
    Why quoted values differ: \emph{the snow line moves}.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Young disk, strong accretion heating: out at $\sim 5$ AU.
      \item As accretion declines and the disk cools, it migrates \textbf{inward}.
      \item The $2.5$--$3.5$ AU range is the location \emph{imprinted on the asteroid belt's} compositional gradient (L4, L12).
    \end{itemize}

    \column{0.42\textwidth}
    \begin{block}{Why it matters}
      \begin{enumerate}
        \item Adding water ice to the rock-and-metal inventory roughly \textbf{triples} the solid mass available.
        \item The \textbf{CHNOPS} elements partition between volatile and refractory phases by local temperature, so \emph{where} a planet forms sets its initial volatile budget.
      \end{enumerate}
    \end{block}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Dr\k{a}\.zkowska et al.\ (2023); Lichtenberg et al.\ (2021); Krijt et al.\ (2023).}
\end{frame}

% ── Öberg hero ──────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \centering
  \makebox[\linewidth][c]{\includegraphics[width=0.95\paperwidth, height=0.85\paperheight, keepaspectratio]{oberg2011_snowlines}}\\
  {\footnotesize Sequential volatile condensation and the resulting C/O ratio across a disk. Reproduced from \"Oberg et al.\ (2011), Fig.~1.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the ice-line sequence}
  Solid curve: C/O of the \emph{gas}. Dashed: C/O of the \emph{grains}. Dotted: the stellar value, solar C/O $= 0.54$.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathrm{H_2O}$ ice line at $\sim 2$ AU: freezing out water \emph{lowers} grain C/O and \emph{raises} gas C/O.
    \item Beyond the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ ice line at $\sim 10$ AU the gas reaches C/O $= 1$ and holds it out to the CO line at $\sim 40$ AU.
    \item Beyond the CO line essentially all C and O are frozen onto grains, so grain C/O returns to the stellar value.
    \item The exact $\mathrm{H_2O}$ line position is model-dependent, $\sim 2$--$3.5$ AU depending on the assumed temperature profile.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \keyresult{A planet inherits the chemistry of its formation zone. Composition is a \emph{location} record, and we will use it as one in L12 and L13.}
\end{frame}

% ── HL Tau hero ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \centering
  \makebox[\linewidth][c]{\includegraphics[width=0.95\paperwidth, height=0.85\paperheight, keepaspectratio]{hl_tau}}\\
  {\footnotesize HL Tauri at $1.3$ mm, ALMA (2014). Credit: ALMA (ESO/NAOJ/NRAO), CC BY 4.0.}

\begin{frame}{Reading HL Tau}
  The 2014 image that reset expectations for how early structure appears.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Star age $\sim 1$ Myr: \textbf{young}, and already highly structured.
    \item Concentric bright rings and dark gaps out to $\sim 100$ AU, resolved at AU scales.
    \item Gaps \emph{may} be carved by forming planets, but ice lines and magnetically driven structures remain viable alternatives.
    \item Either way: the dust is not smoothly distributed, and the conditions for planet formation are set early.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize ALMA Partnership et al.\ (2015).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \centering
  \makebox[\linewidth][c]{\includegraphics[width=0.95\paperwidth, height=0.85\paperheight, keepaspectratio]{dsharp_composite}}\\
  {\footnotesize The 20 disks of the ALMA DSHARP survey at $240$ GHz. Reproduced from Andrews et al.\ (2018), Fig.~3.}

\begin{frame}{Reading the DSHARP gallery}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item 20 nearby disks at $240$ GHz ($1.25$ mm), $\sim 5$ AU resolution.
    \item Beam sizes (lower left) and $10$ AU scale bars (lower right) in each panel; the colour stretch is chosen to bring out faint substructure.
    \item Rings and gaps appear in \emph{nearly every} disk, not just HL Tau.
    \item \textbf{Caveat that matters}: the sample was deliberately biased toward the largest and brightest disks. Not every disk need be this richly structured.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \keyresult{Substructure is the norm among bright disks, and it appears early. Whatever forms planets is already at work in the first Myr.}
\end{frame}

% ── Disk lifetimes ──────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The deadline: disk lifetimes}
  Infrared-excess surveys of clusters at different ages measure the fraction of stars still holding an optically thick \emph{inner} disk.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\sim 80\%$ at $1$ Myr, falling to $< 10\%$ by $\sim 6$ Myr.
    \item Half of all stars lose their inner disks within $\sim 3$ Myr.
    \item Essentially all disks are gone by $\sim 6$ Myr.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  Dispersal is driven by viscous accretion onto the star, photoevaporation by stellar UV and X-rays, and probably magnetohydrodynamic winds.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \keyresult{A hard deadline for gas-giant formation: no core massive enough before the gas goes, no giant planet. This single constraint drives most of the theory that follows.}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Haisch, Lada \& Lada (2001).}
\end{frame}

\section{From dust to planetesimals}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ── The scale problem ───────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Twelve orders of magnitude}
  The raw material is sub-micrometre dust: silicates, metal oxides, and, beyond the snow line, ices.
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \begin{center}
    \large
    $1\ \mu\mathrm{m}\ \longrightarrow\ 1000\ \mathrm{km}$ \qquad is a factor $\sim 10^{12}$ in size.
  \end{center}
  \vskip8pt\relax
  The physics changes character at almost every decade along the way:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contact forces} (van der Waals) hold the smallest aggregates together.
    \item \textbf{Aerodynamics} (gas drag) dominates the pebble-to-boulder range.
    \item \textbf{Self-gravity} takes over only above roughly a kilometre.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  There is no single mechanism that spans the whole range, which is why this section has several.
\end{frame}

% ── Grain growth ────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Grain growth: sticking and settling}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth, height=0.62\textheight, keepaspectratio]{blum2008_aggregate_growth}
    \source{Blum \& Wurm (2008), Fig.~2}

    \column{0.49\textwidth}
    Grains collide through Brownian motion, turbulence, and settling.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item At $\lesssim 1$ m s$^{-1}$ they \textbf{stick}, held by van der Waals forces.
      \item The products are \textbf{fractal aggregates}: fluffy, porous, $D \approx 1.4$.
      \item \textbf{Inset}: $1.9$ µm $\mathrm{SiO_2}$ monomers aggregated in microgravity.
      \item \textbf{Main panel}: aggregate mass vs time, in monomer units, against a Smoluchowski model.
      \item Efficient to mm and cm sizes. Then it stops.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ── Growth barriers ─────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Three barriers stop incremental growth}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bouncing barrier} ($\sim$ mm). Slightly faster collisions compact the aggregates, and compacted aggregates bounce instead of sticking.
    \vskip3pt\relax
    \item \textbf{Fragmentation barrier} ($\sim$ cm). Above $\sim 1$--$10$ m s$^{-1}$, depending on material, aggregates shatter. Silicates are fragile; ices survive faster impacts.
    \vskip3pt\relax
    \item \textbf{Radial drift barrier} ($\sim$ metre). Gas is partly pressure-supported, so it orbits slightly slower than Keplerian. Solids feel a \textbf{headwind}, lose angular momentum, and spiral inward. The drift rate \emph{peaks} for metre-sized boulders, which reach the star in $\sim 100$--$1000$ years.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \keyresult{The metre-size barrier is the central problem: growth by sticking is slower than removal by drift, exactly in the size range you must cross.}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Birnstiel et al.\ (2016).}
\end{frame}

% ── Growth-barrier hero ─────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \centering
  \makebox[\linewidth][c]{\includegraphics[width=0.95\paperwidth, height=0.85\paperheight, keepaspectratio]{drazkowska2023_growth_barriers}}\\
  {\footnotesize Maximum grain size vs orbital distance in a standard disk model. Adapted from Dr\k{a}\.zkowska et al.\ (2023), Fig.~4.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the growth-barrier map}
  Axes: orbital distance against the largest grain size that can survive there.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Red curves}: where collision speeds reach the bouncing ($v_{\mathrm{th}} = 1$ cm s$^{-1}$) or fragmentation ($1$ and $10$ m s$^{-1}$) thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Blue curve}: the radial-drift limit. Above it, grains drift inward faster than they grow.
    \item \textbf{Grey contours}: constant Stokes number, i.e.\ how tightly a particle couples to the gas.
    \item \textbf{Black curves}: representative growth histories, running into whichever limit is lowest.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  Sticking stalls between tens of µm and dm; planetesimals are kilometres.
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \keyresult{The gap is not marginal. Incremental sticking is off by many orders of magnitude, so a \emph{collective} mechanism is required.}
\end{frame}

% ── Streaming instability ───────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The streaming instability}
  The way out is not stronger glue but \textbf{collective aerodynamics}.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  When the local dust-to-gas ratio approaches unity, through settling to the midplane or accumulation at pressure bumps, the drag coupling changes character:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item A local clump of particles \textbf{drags the gas along}, accelerating it toward the Keplerian velocity.
    \item Faster gas means a \textbf{weaker headwind}, so the clump's own drift slows.
    \item Particles drifting in from further out \textbf{catch up and join} the clump.
    \item Positive feedback: dense filaments form, and where they are dense enough they \textbf{collapse under self-gravity}.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  The product is a \textbf{planetesimal}, radius tens to a few hundred km, formed directly. The awkward metre-to-kilometre range is simply skipped.
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Johansen et al.\ (2007); Simon et al.\ (2016).}
\end{frame}

% ── Simon hero ──────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \centering
  \makebox[\linewidth][c]{\includegraphics[width=0.95\paperwidth, height=0.85\paperheight, keepaspectratio]{simon2016_streaming}}\\
  {\footnotesize Streaming-instability simulation: particle surface density in a local shearing box. Reproduced from Simon et al.\ (2016), Fig.~3.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the streaming-instability snapshots}
  Four snapshots, time increasing from top left to bottom right. Colour: particle surface density normalised to its mean, logarithmic scale. Axes in gas scale heights $H$.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early}: drag feedback has already organised the pebbles into loose, azimuthally extended \textbf{filaments}.
    \item \textbf{Then}: the filaments merge and sharpen into one dominant dense band.
    \item \textbf{Then}: the filaments fragment along their length under self-gravity.
    \item \textbf{Final panel}: gravitationally bound clumps (red points) have collapsed into planetesimals.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  Note the scale: this is a \emph{local} box, a few scale heights across, not a whole disk. The instability is a midplane process.
\end{frame}

% ── Pebble accretion ────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Pebble accretion}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth, height=0.62\textheight, keepaspectratio]{lambrechts2012_pebble_accretion}
    \source{Adapted from Lambrechts \& Johansen (2012)}

    \column{0.51\textwidth}
    Large planetesimals sweep up the abundant mm- to cm-sized pebbles.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Frame co-moving with the embryo; axes in Hill radii $r_{\mathrm{H}}$; red circle is the Hill sphere.
      \item Line styles: friction times $\tau_{\mathrm{f}} = 0.01$, $0.1$, $1$, i.e.\ weaker gas coupling.
      \item Gas drag makes pebbles entering the Hill sphere \textbf{spiral in} instead of passing by.
      \item The effective cross-section far exceeds the geometric one.
      \item Cores reach $\sim 10\,\Mearth$ \emph{within} the disk lifetime.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ── Pebble isolation ────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Pebble accretion switches itself off}
  Pebble accretion is \textbf{self-limiting}, and how it stops matters as much as how it works.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{block}{Pebble isolation mass}
    A sufficiently massive planet raises a \textbf{pressure bump} in the gas just outside its orbit. Inward-drifting pebbles pile up there and stop: the planet's supply is cut off.
  \end{block}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  The consequence is not local:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The supply to the \emph{entire disk interior to that orbit} is cut off at the same time, so one planet changes the solid budget everywhere inside it.
    \item A leading explanation for the isotopic split between inner and outer solar-system material (L12).
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Dr\k{a}\.zkowska et al.\ (2023).}
\end{frame}

% ── Preferential sites ──────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Planets do not form everywhere at once}
  Formation concentrates where solids concentrate:
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pressure bumps}: a local gas-pressure maximum halts radial drift, so solids accumulate instead of passing through.
    \item \textbf{Ice lines}: sublimation and recondensation enhance the local solid density and change grain stickiness.
    \item \textbf{Dead-zone edges}: boundaries between turbulent and quiescent regions trap material.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \keyresult{A non-uniform distribution of formation sites is one reason exoplanetary architectures are so diverse (L13).}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Dr\k{a}\.zkowska et al.\ (2023).}
\end{frame}

  Horizontal axis: body size, from sub-µm dust to gas giants. Reference bodies (Ceres, Moon, Mars, Earth, Jupiter) mark the scale.

  \begin{itemize}

\section{Building planets: runaway growth to gas giants}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ── Runaway growth ──────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Runaway growth}
  Once planetesimals exist, \textbf{gravity} takes over from aerodynamics. A larger body bends incoming trajectories toward itself, so it collides with far more than its geometric cross-section:
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \[
    \sigma_{\mathrm{eff}} = \pi R^2 \left(1 + \frac{v_{\mathrm{esc}}^2}{v_{\infty}^2}\right),
    \qquad v_{\mathrm{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}
  \]
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item In a dynamically cold swarm ($v_{\infty} \ll v_{\mathrm{esc}}$) the \textbf{focusing factor} is enormous.
    \item $v_{\mathrm{esc}}$ grows with mass, so \emph{the biggest body grows fastest}: the size gap widens with time.
    \item At $1$ AU, a single body reaches roughly \textbf{lunar mass within $\sim 10^5$ years}.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  Growth toward Mars-mass embryos continues more slowly; the final terrestrial planets take far longer still (L4).
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Kokubo \& Ida (1998).}
\end{frame}

% ── Oligarchic growth ───────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Oligarchic growth}
  Runaway growth destroys its own precondition.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The growing bodies \textbf{stir up} the surrounding planetesimals, raising $v_{\infty}$.
    \item Higher $v_{\infty}$ shrinks the focusing factor, so the runaway slows into the \textbf{oligarchic regime}.
    \item A few dozen comparable-mass \textbf{oligarchs} each dominate a feeding zone, separated by $\sim 5$--$10$ mutual Hill radii.
    \item Each grows until it reaches its \textbf{isolation mass}: all material in its feeding zone consumed.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{block}{The problem this leaves behind}
    In the inner solar system the isolation mass is only $\sim 0.01$--$0.1\,\Mearth$, an order of magnitude short of Earth. Something later must merge the oligarchs.
  \end{block}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Kokubo \& Ida (1998).}
\end{frame}

% ── Core accretion ──────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Core accretion: how to build a giant}
  The dominant model for giant-planet formation, in four steps:
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item A \textbf{solid core} grows from planetesimals and pebbles \emph{beyond the snow line}, where solids are abundant.
    \item At a critical core mass of $\sim 10$--$20\,\Mearth$, gravity can bind a substantial \textbf{gas envelope}.
    \item The envelope contracts and cools, admitting more gas: \textbf{runaway gas accretion}. Hundreds of Earth masses of H and He within $\sim 10^5$ years.
    \item Accretion ends when the planet \textbf{opens a gap} or the disk \textbf{dissipates}.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \keyresult{This explains the solar system's giants in one sentence: Jupiter and Saturn reached critical core mass before dispersal; Uranus and Neptune reached it too late, after most gas was gone.}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Pollack et al.\ (1996).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[plain]

  {\footnotesize Growth of a giant planet by core accretion at $5.2$ AU. Reproduced from Helled et al.\ (2014), Fig.~1; simulation from Lissauer et al.\ (2009).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the core-accretion curves}
  Mass against time for a classic simulation of Jupiter's formation at $5.2$ AU.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Solid line}: core mass. \textbf{Dash-dotted}: envelope mass. \textbf{Dashed}: total.
    \item \textbf{Phase 1}: rapid core growth to $\sim 10\,\Mearth$.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2}: a long plateau of slow, quasi-hydrostatic envelope growth. This phase sets the clock, and it is what must fit inside the disk lifetime.
    \item \textbf{Phase 3}: runaway gas accretion, beginning at the \textbf{crossover point} where envelope mass overtakes core mass, at $2.3$ Myr in this run, reaching a Jupiter mass by $3$ Myr.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \keyresult{Compare $3$ Myr here with the disk-lifetime plot: giant-planet formation only just fits inside the deadline.}
\end{frame}

% ── Gravitational instability ───────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Gravitational instability: the alternative pathway}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.50\textwidth}
    A massive disk that cools rapidly enough can \textbf{fragment directly} into self-gravitating clumps, which contract into giant planets. No solid core needed first.
    \vskip6pt\relax
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Fast: orbital timescales, $\sim 10^3$ years.
      \item But it needs high disk mass \emph{and} efficient cooling.
    \end{itemize}

    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \begin{block}{Where those conditions hold}
      Only at large orbital distances, $\gtrsim 30$--$50$ AU, in unusually massive disks.
    \end{block}
    \vskip8pt\relax
    So it is today regarded as a pathway for \textbf{massive companions on wide orbits}, not for the solar system's giants.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Boss (1997); Kratter \& Lodato (2016).}
\end{frame}

% ── Giant impacts ───────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The giant impact phase}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth, height=0.62\textheight, keepaspectratio]{giant_impact}
    \source{NASA/JPL-Caltech (artist's concept)}

    \column{0.53\textwidth}
    After the disk disperses, there is no gas drag left to damp eccentricities.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Embryo orbits grow eccentric and start crossing.
      \item $\sim 10$--$100$ Myr of chaotic \textbf{giant impacts} merges the oligarchs into the final terrestrial planets.
      \item The \textbf{Moon-forming impact}, proto-Earth struck by the Mars-sized body Theia, is our most dramatic example.
    \end{itemize}
    \vskip4pt\relax
    This is the process that finally bridges the isolation-mass gap.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ── Geophysical consequences ────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Formation is also a geophysical process}
  Assembly is not just bookkeeping of mass. The energy delivered changes what the planet \emph{is}.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Accretion and giant impacts \textbf{melt} the planet: a global \textbf{magma ocean}.
    \item Molten, dense metallic iron sinks and light silicates float: \textbf{core-mantle differentiation}.
    \item Short-lived radionuclides, above all $^{26}\mathrm{Al}$ (half-life $0.72$ Myr), melt even \emph{small planetesimals}, so differentiation can begin before assembly finishes.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \keyresult{The thermal and chemical state inherited from formation shapes everything downstream: magnetic field, volcanism, outgassing, habitability (L3, L4).}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Lichtenberg et al.\ (2023).}
\end{frame}

\section{Kepler's laws and orbital elements}

% ── Kepler's three laws ─────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Kepler's three laws}
  Distilled by Kepler from Tycho Brahe's observations, early 17th century. Empirical first, explained later.
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The law of ellipses.} Each planet moves on an ellipse with the Sun at one \emph{focus}, not the centre.
    \vskip2pt\relax
    \item \textbf{The law of equal areas.} The Sun-planet line sweeps equal areas in equal times: fastest at perihelion, slowest at aphelion.
    \vskip2pt\relax
    \item \textbf{The harmonic law.} $P^2 \propto a^3$, which in Newton's form reads
    \[
      P^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G(M_1 + M_2)}\, a^3
    \]
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  Newton showed all three follow from one universal law of gravitation (L1).
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \keyresult{The third law is the workhorse of the whole course: it turns a \emph{measured period} into a \emph{mass}.}
\end{frame}

% ── Kepler figure ───────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The three laws, geometrically}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \column{0.50\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth, height=0.70\textheight, keepaspectratio]{kepler_laws}
    \source{Hankwang, CC BY 2.5}

    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{(1)} The Sun sits at a focus. The empty focus has no physical meaning, which is why the ellipse was so hard to accept.
      \item \textbf{(2)} The shaded wedges have \emph{equal area}, so they are swept in equal time: short and fat near perihelion, long and thin near aphelion.
      \item \textbf{(3)} $P^2 \propto a^3$ ties different planets' orbits to each other.
      \item Law 2 is angular-momentum conservation in disguise. We use it in the derivation shortly.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ── Orbital elements table ──────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Six orbital elements}
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{lcl}
    \toprule
    Element & Symbol & Meaning \\
    \midrule
    Semi-major axis            & $a$      & Size of the orbit \\
    Eccentricity               & $e$      & Shape ($e = 0$ circular, $0 < e < 1$ elliptical) \\
    Inclination                & $i$      & Tilt of the orbital plane vs a reference plane \\
    Longitude of ascending node& $\Omega$ & Where the orbit crosses the reference plane \\
    Argument of perihelion     & $\omega$ & Orientation of the ellipse within its plane \\
    True anomaly               & $\nu$    & Where the body is, right now \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The first five are \textbf{constant} for a Keplerian orbit: they define size, shape, and orientation.
    \item Only the sixth changes with time.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  Perturbations, resonances, tides, and migration all act by \emph{slowly changing the first five}. That is the subject of the rest of this lecture.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The four angular elements}

      \item \textbf{Grey}: the reference plane, usually the ecliptic for solar-system work.
      \item The orbital plane is tilted from it by the inclination $i$.
      \item $\Omega$ locates the \textbf{ascending node}, where the body crosses northward.
      \item $\omega$ rotates the ellipse \emph{within} its plane (here ``argument of periapsis'', the general term).
      \item $\nu$ finally locates the body along the orbit.
      \item With $a$ and $e$ for size and shape, the description is complete.
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the solar system's elements tell us}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.50\textwidth}
    Most planets have
    \begin{itemize}
      \item nearly circular orbits, $e < 0.1$;
      \item low inclinations, $i \lesssim 3.5^{\circ}$.
    \end{itemize}
    \vskip6pt\relax

    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \begin{block}{The exceptions are the interesting part}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Mercury}: $e = 0.206$, $i = 7.0^{\circ}$.
        \item \textbf{Pluto}: $e = 0.25$, $i = 17^{\circ}$.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{block}
    \vskip8pt\relax
    Both point to a more complicated dynamical history than ``formed here, stayed here''. Pluto's case we solve later today, with resonances.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{The two-body problem and vis-viva}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ── Two-body reduction ──────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Reducing two bodies to one}
  Two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ interacting through gravity alone. In the centre-of-mass frame this becomes a \emph{single} body of \textbf{reduced mass}
  \[
    \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}
  \]
  moving in the field of a fixed central mass $M = m_1 + m_2$.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  The total orbital energy is then
  \[
    E = \frac{1}{2}\mu v^2 - \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r}
  \]
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item For a planet around a star, $m_2 \ll m_1$, so $\mu \to m_2$ and $M \to m_1$: the familiar one-body picture.
    \item The reduction is exact, not an approximation. It is what makes the two-body problem solvable in closed form, and the three-body problem not.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ── Energy depends only on a ────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The central result: energy depends only on $a$}
  \begin{center}
    \[
      \boxed{\;E = -\frac{G m_1 m_2}{2a}\;}
    \]
  \end{center}
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item No $e$ anywhere on the right-hand side.
    \item Two orbits with the same semi-major axis have the \textbf{same total energy}, however different their shapes.
    \item A circular orbit and a wildly eccentric one with the same $a$ are energetically \emph{equivalent}: eccentricity only redistributes kinetic and potential energy \emph{along} the orbit.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \keyresult{This is why $a$ is the natural coordinate for everything dynamical: migration changes energy, resonance and tides trade $e$ against it.}
\end{frame}

% ── Vis-viva ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The vis-viva equation}
  Combining the total energy with the instantaneous kinetic plus potential energy gives \emph{vis viva}, Latin for ``living force'':
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \[
    \boxed{\; v^2 = G(m_1 + m_2)\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right) \;}
  \]
  \vskip4pt\relax
  For $m_2 \ll m_1$: $v^2 = GM\left(2/r - 1/a\right)$, the speed at \emph{any} point of \emph{any} orbit.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  Two special cases worth memorising:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Circular orbit} ($r = a$): $\quad v_{\mathrm{circ}} = \sqrt{GM/a}$
    \item \textbf{Escape} ($a \to \infty$, $E = 0$): $\quad v_{\mathrm{esc}} = \sqrt{2GM/r}$
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \keyresult{Escape velocity is exactly $\sqrt{2}$ times the circular velocity at the same distance. Worth remembering, and worth checking on the exam.}
\end{frame}

\sectionimage{halley}{Comet 1P/Halley's nucleus, Giotto HMC (1986). Credit: ESA / MPAe Lindau}
\section{Blackboard derivation: the vis-viva equation}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ── Setup ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Setup}
  A planet of mass $m$ orbiting a star of mass $M$, with $m \ll M$. Orbit has semi-major axis $a$ and eccentricity $e$. At any moment the planet is at distance $r$ and moves with speed $v$.
  \vskip8pt\relax
  Total mechanical energy is conserved:
  \[
    E = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{r} = \mathrm{constant}
  \]
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \textbf{Goal:} express $E$ in terms of $a$ alone, then solve for $v(r)$.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  The trick throughout is to evaluate the conserved quantities at the two points where the geometry is simplest.
\end{frame}

% ── Step 1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Step 1: evaluate the energy at perihelion and aphelion}
  At those two points the distances are fixed by the geometry of the ellipse:
  \[
    r_p = a(1 - e), \qquad r_a = a(1 + e)
  \]
  \vskip4pt\relax
  Energy conservation between them:
  \[
    \frac{1}{2}mv_p^2 - \frac{GMm}{a(1-e)} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}mv_a^2 - \frac{GMm}{a(1+e)}
  \]
  \vskip6pt\relax
  Two unknown speeds, one equation. We need a second relation between $v_p$ and $v_a$.
\end{frame}

% ── Step 2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Step 2: use angular momentum}
  At perihelion and aphelion, and \emph{only} there, the velocity is purely tangential. So the angular momentum is just $m v r$:
  \[
    L = m\, v_p\, r_p = m\, v_a\, r_a
    \quad \Longrightarrow \quad
    v_p\, a(1-e) = v_a\, a(1+e)
  \]
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \[
    \frac{v_p}{v_a} = \frac{1+e}{1-e}
  \]
  \vskip6pt\relax
  This is Kepler's second law doing the work: equal areas in equal times \emph{is} conservation of angular momentum.
\end{frame}

% ── Step 3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Step 3: solve for the total energy}
  Substituting the speed ratio back into the energy equation and simplifying (the algebra is a good exercise; do it):
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \[
    \boxed{\; E = -\frac{GMm}{2a} \;}
  \]
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The eccentricity has \textbf{cancelled}.
    \item $E < 0$ for any bound orbit, and $E \to 0$ as $a \to \infty$.
    \item A remarkable result for how little it costs: two conservation laws, evaluated at two convenient points.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ── Step 4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Step 4: write the vis-viva equation}
  Set the total energy equal to the instantaneous kinetic plus potential energy at an arbitrary point:
  \[
    \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{r} = -\frac{GMm}{2a}
  \]
  \vskip4pt\relax
  Divide through by $m/2$ and rearrange:
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \[
    \boxed{\; v^2 = GM\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right) \;}
  \]
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \keyresult{This generalises L1: setting gravity equal to the centripetal acceleration gave $v^2 = GM/r$, which is vis-viva for the special case $r = a$.}
\end{frame}

% ── Application: Earth ──────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Application 1: Earth}
  For Earth, $a = 1$ AU $= 1.496 \times 10^{11}$ m and $e = 0.0167$:
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    Point & Distance $r$ & Speed \\
    \midrule
    Perihelion & $a(1-e) = 1.471 \times 10^{11}$ m & $v_p = 30.29$ km s$^{-1}$ \\
    Aphelion   & $a(1+e) = 1.521 \times 10^{11}$ m & $v_a = 29.29$ km s$^{-1}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Variation of only about $\pm 0.5$ km s$^{-1}$ around the mean, because the orbit is nearly circular.
    \item Mean orbital speed $v \approx 2\pi a / P \approx 29.8$ km s$^{-1}$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ── Application: Halley ─────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Application 2: Halley's comet}
  Same equation, an orbit as different as the solar system provides: $a = 17.83$ AU, $e = 0.967$.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    Point & Distance $r$ & Speed \\
    \midrule
    Perihelion & $a(1-e) = 0.588$ AU & $v_p = 54.5$ km s$^{-1}$ \\

    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \normalsize
  Halley crosses the inner solar system at nearly twice Earth's speed, then crawls beyond Neptune's orbit at under $1$ km s$^{-1}$.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \keyresult{A factor $\sim 60$ in speed on a single orbit, from one equation and one number: the eccentricity.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[plain]

  Speed against distance for the two orbits just computed. Filled circles mark perihelion and aphelion.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Earth}: a short, almost flat segment near $30$ km s$^{-1}$. Nearly circular means nearly constant speed.
    \item \textbf{Halley}: a steep curve spanning $54.5$ km s$^{-1}$ down to $0.91$ km s$^{-1}$.
    \item Both curves obey the \emph{same} equation with the \emph{same} $GM$. Only $a$ and $e$ differ.
    \item The curves never cross: at any shared $r$, $v^2$ differs by the \emph{constant} $GM(1/a_{\oplus} - 1/a_{\mathrm{H}})$, so the larger-$a$ orbit is faster. Passing Earth's orbit, Halley moves at $41.5$ km s$^{-1}$ against Earth's $29.8$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\sectionimage{galilean_moons}{The Galilean moons: Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto. Credit: NASA/JPL}
\section{Orbital resonances}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ── MMR concept ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Mean-motion resonances}
  When two orbital periods stand in a ratio of \textbf{small integers}, the bodies are in a \textbf{mean-motion resonance} (MMR).
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A $p$:$q$ resonance (with $p > q$) means the inner body completes $p$ orbits while the outer completes $q$.
    \item Conjunctions then always happen at the \emph{same orbital phases}.
    \item So the mutual gravitational kicks \textbf{add coherently} over many orbits, instead of averaging to zero.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \keyresult{Resonance is a lever: a tiny force applied always at the same phase produces a large cumulative effect. Depending on geometry it can either protect an orbit or destroy it.}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Murray \& Dermott (1999).}
\end{frame}

% ── Resonances in the solar system ──────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Resonances are everywhere in the solar system}
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    Resonance & Bodies & What it does \\
    \midrule
    3:2               & Pluto--Neptune    & Pluto completes 2 orbits per 3 of Neptune \\
    Near 5:2          & Jupiter--Saturn   & ``Great inequality'': long-period perturbations \\
    3:1, 5:2, 7:3, 2:1& Asteroid belt     & Kirkwood gaps, cleared of asteroids (L12) \\
    1:1               & Jupiter Trojans   & Co-orbital swarms at the L4 and L5 points \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip10pt\relax
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Protective}: the 3:2 with Neptune is why Pluto can cross Neptune's orbit and never meet it.
    \item \textbf{Destructive}: the Kirkwood gaps are resonances that \emph{pump eccentricity} until the asteroid is removed.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  Same physics, opposite outcome, decided by the geometry of the encounter.
\end{frame}

% ── Laplace resonance ───────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The Laplace resonance}
  Jupiter's three inner Galilean moons, the most famous resonance in the solar system.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    Moon & Period (days) & Ratio to Io \\
    \midrule
    Io       & $1.769$ & $1$ \\
    Europa   & $3.551$ & $\approx 2$ \\
    Ganymede & $7.155$ & $\approx 4$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Periods in a $1 : 2 : 4$ ratio, equivalently mean motions $n_{\mathrm{Io}} : n_{\mathrm{Eur}} : n_{\mathrm{Gan}} = 4 : 2 : 1$.
    \item A genuine \textbf{three-body} resonance, maintained by tidal interaction with Jupiter.
    \item Its key consequence: it \textbf{forces non-zero eccentricities}. Io's is pinned at $e \approx 0.004$.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Peale, Cassen \& Reynolds (1979).}
\end{frame}

% ── Laplace hero ────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \centering
  \makebox[\linewidth][c]{\includegraphics[width=0.95\paperwidth, height=0.85\paperheight, keepaspectratio]{laplace_resonance}}\\
  {\footnotesize The Laplace resonance of Io, Europa, and Ganymede. Course-original figure.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the Laplace resonance}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Panel (a)}: the three orbits to scale, drawn at a Europa--Ganymede conjunction. The resonance locks the phases so that Io is always on the \emph{opposite side of Jupiter} at those conjunctions.
    \item Consequence, and a common misconception to avoid: a \textbf{triple conjunction never occurs}. The three moons are never lined up together.
    \item \textbf{Panel (b)}: the periods in their $1 : 2 : 4$ ratio. The pattern of successive conjunctions repeats every Ganymede period, that is, every four Io orbits.
    \item The periodic kicks arrive always at the same phase, which is what sustains Io's $e \approx 0.004$ against tidal damping.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \keyresult{An eccentricity of $0.004$ sounds negligible. It is the reason Io is the most volcanically active body in the solar system.}
\end{frame}

\section{Tides, locking, and the Roche limit}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ── Tidal force ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The tidal force}
  A tidal force arises whenever a gravitational field \textbf{varies across the extent} of an extended body.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  For a moon of radius $R_s$ at distance $d$ from a planet of mass $M_p$, the acceleration difference between the moon's centre and its near side is
  \[
    \Delta a_{\mathrm{tidal}} \approx \frac{2 G M_p R_s}{d^3}
  \]
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The near side is pulled harder than the centre, the far side less hard: the body is \textbf{stretched} along the line to the planet.
    \item Note the exponent: $1/d^3$, not $1/d^2$. Tides fall off much faster than gravity itself.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \keyresult{The steep distance dependence is why tides are irrelevant for most of a planetary system and utterly dominant close in.}
\end{frame}

% ── Bulges and dissipation ──────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Bulges, torques, and heat}
  The tidal force raises \textbf{two bulges}, one facing the perturber and one on the opposite side.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item If the body rotates at a different rate than it orbits, which is the generic starting condition, the bulges are \textbf{carried away} from the line joining the two bodies.
    \item That misalignment gives the perturber a lever arm: a \textbf{torque} that exchanges angular momentum between spin and orbit.
    \item Continually deforming the body \textbf{dissipates energy as heat}: \textbf{tidal heating}.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  Tidal heating powers Io's volcanism and may maintain the subsurface oceans of Europa and Enceladus (L3, L11).
\end{frame}

% ── Tidal locking ───────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \centering
  \makebox[\linewidth][c]{\includegraphics[width=0.95\paperwidth, height=0.85\paperheight, keepaspectratio]{tidal_locking}}\\
  {\footnotesize Free versus synchronous rotation. Course-original schematic.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tidal locking}
  Over time the tidal torque slows the faster-spinning body until its rotation period equals its orbital period: \textbf{synchronous rotation}.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Panel (a)}, free rotation: spin and orbital periods differ, so the moon shows different faces to the planet (P) over one orbit. The black surface marker and the body-fixed axis (red arrow) decouple from the direction to P.
    \item \textbf{Panel (b)}, \textbf{synchronous rotation}: the periods are equal, so the marker, and with it the tidal bulge, always faces the planet.
    \item With the bulge aligned, the torque vanishes and the configuration is stable.
    \item The Moon reached this state through billions of years of dissipation. All four Galilean moons are locked to Jupiter; most large moons in the solar system are synchronous rotators.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Murray \& Dermott (1999).}
\end{frame}

% ── Locking is not the end of heating ───────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Locked does not mean cold}
  A common and consequential misconception: that synchronous rotation switches tidal dissipation off.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item On a \textbf{circular} orbit it essentially does. Bulge aligned, nothing flexes, no heat.
    \item On an \textbf{eccentric} orbit it does not. Distance and apparent bulge direction both vary over each orbit, so a locked body \textbf{keeps flexing}.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  Exactly Io's situation: locked to Jupiter, but with $e \approx 0.004$ pinned by the Laplace resonance.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{block}{How fast does locking happen?}
    The timescale scales as $d^6 / M_p^2$: steeply with distance, inversely with the square of the primary's mass. Close-in moons of massive planets lock almost immediately; distant, small companions may never lock at all.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

% ── Roche limit ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The Roche limit}
  There is a minimum distance at which a self-gravitating body survives the tide of a larger one. Inside it, tides beat self-gravity and the body is torn apart.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  For a \emph{fluid} satellite of density $\rho_s$ orbiting a planet of radius $R_p$ and density $\rho_p$:
  \[
    d_R \approx 2.46\, R_p \left(\frac{\rho_p}{\rho_s}\right)^{1/3}
  \]
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Note what does \emph{not} appear: the satellite's \textbf{size}. Only the densities and the primary's radius matter.
    \item The numerical coefficient depends on the assumed satellite rheology, which is the subject of the next slide.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  We derive this in full in L11.
\end{frame}

% ── Roche hero ──────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \centering
  \makebox[\linewidth][c]{\includegraphics[width=0.95\paperwidth, height=0.85\paperheight, keepaspectratio]{roche_geometry}}\\
  {\footnotesize Roche limit geometry, and the tidal-versus-self-gravity crossing for a $200$ km icy body at Saturn. Course-original figure.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the Roche geometry}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Panel (a)}: a fluid satellite is rounded at a safe distance. At $d_R$ the differential tidal acceleration between its centre and its surface exceeds its own self-gravity, and it is stretched and torn apart along the line to the primary.
    \item \textbf{Panel (b)}: tidal acceleration compared with surface self-gravity for a $200$ km icy body, against distance from Saturn in units of $R_p$.
    \item The simple \textbf{crossing} of the two curves gives the \emph{rigid} limit, $d_R^{\mathrm{rigid}} \approx 1.11\,R_p$ for $\rho_s = 1000$ kg m$^{-3}$.
    \item The textbook \emph{fluid} limit, $d_R \approx 2.17\,R_p$ (red dotted), lies further out because it adds the tidal \textbf{deformation} of the satellite, which weakens it further.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \keyresult{Two limits, a factor of two apart. Which one applies depends on whether the body can hold its shape, so real bodies fall between them.}
\end{frame}

% ── Saturn numbers ──────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Evaluating the Roche limit at Saturn}
  With $R_p = 58{,}232$ km, $\rho_p = 687$ kg m$^{-3}$, and an icy satellite $\rho_s \approx 1000$ kg m$^{-3}$:
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \[
    d_R \approx 2.46 \times 58{,}232\ \mathrm{km} \times \left(\frac{687}{1000}\right)^{1/3}
    \approx 126{,}000\ \mathrm{km} \approx 2.17\, R_p
  \]
  \vskip6pt\relax
  Compare with the actual ring system:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Main rings (D through A): $\sim 67{,}000$ km to $137{,}000$ km from Saturn's centre.
    \item The narrow F ring lies just beyond, at $\sim 140{,}000$ km.
    \item The dense \textbf{B and C rings sit well inside} the fluid limit, as expected.
    \item But \textbf{much of the A ring's width lies outside} it.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  Two reasons the simple number is not the whole story: the A ring's sharp outer edge is held by a \textbf{7:6 resonance with Janus}, and the particles are \textbf{porous}, so their density is below solid ice, which pushes the effective limit outward.
\end{frame}

\section{Planetary migration}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ── Planet-disk interaction ─────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Planets do not stay where they form}
  A planet embedded in a gaseous disk exchanges angular momentum with it, through two distinct channels.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lindblad resonances.} The planet excites spiral density waves at the radii where the disk's orbital frequency differs from the planet's by $\pm \kappa/m$, with $\kappa$ the local epicyclic frequency and $m$ a positive integer. The waves carry angular momentum away.
    \item \textbf{Corotation resonances.} Material co-orbiting with the planet exchanges angular momentum along \textbf{horseshoe-shaped streamlines}.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  The \emph{net} torque decides whether the planet moves in or out, and how fast.
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \keyresult{Migration is not an exotic add-on. Any planet massive enough to perturb the disk is being pushed by it.}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Paardekooper et al.\ (2023).}
\end{frame}

% ── Types of migration ──────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Three types of migration}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type I.} A low-mass planet (below the gap-opening threshold, a few tens of Earth masses) stays fully embedded and migrates on the imbalance between inner and outer Lindblad torques. In simple models the outer torque slightly wins: \emph{inward} migration on $\sim 10^5$ yr.
    \vskip3pt\relax
    \item \textbf{Type II.} A massive planet, roughly Jupiter-mass and up, \textbf{opens a gap}: an annulus cleared of gas. It then migrates on the slower viscous timescale, $\sim 10^5$--$10^6$ yr, as the disk drains onto the star.
    \vskip3pt\relax
    \item \textbf{Type III.} In massive disks with a sharp density gradient near the planet, a runaway sets in when the \textbf{co-orbital mass deficit} approaches the planet's mass. Very fast, either direction.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \textbf{The Type I problem}: $10^5$ yr is uncomfortably short against a $\sim 3$ Myr disk lifetime. Taken literally, cores fall into the star before they finish growing; extra physics (thermal effects, magnetic fields, disk structure) must slow or reverse it.
\end{frame}

% ── Migration hero ──────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \centering
  \makebox[\linewidth][c]{\includegraphics[width=0.95\paperwidth, height=0.85\paperheight, keepaspectratio]{paardekooper2023_disk_flow}}\\
  {\footnotesize Disk surface-density response to an embedded Neptune-mass planet. Adapted from Paardekooper et al.\ (2023), Fig.~2.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the disk-flow figure}
  A planet of mass ratio $q = 10^{-4}$, Neptune-like, on a circular orbit.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Top panel}: the planet launches a one-armed \textbf{spiral density wave}. Colour is surface density relative to unperturbed.
    \item Note what is absent: \textbf{no gap}. At this mass the planet perturbs the disk without clearing it, which is the definition of the Type I regime.
    \item \textbf{Bottom panel}: zoom on the co-orbital region, with schematic streamlines showing the \textbf{horseshoe trajectories} and the flows feeding the spiral wave.
    \item The imbalance between inner and outer wave torques, plus the corotation torque from the horseshoe region, sets both the direction and the speed.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ── Nice model ──────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The Nice model}
  Migration continues after the gas goes: the giants scatter a remnant \textbf{planetesimal disk}.
  \vskip2pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The giants formed \textbf{more compact}, the outermost ice giant well inside Neptune's present $30$ AU orbit, until a dynamical instability rearranged the system.
    \item \textbf{Original version}: triggered when Jupiter and Saturn slowly crossed their mutual \textbf{2:1} resonance.
    \item \textbf{Modern version}: the belt's structure and the near-circular terrestrial orbits require the Jupiter--Saturn period ratio to change \textbf{discontinuously}, most naturally if a \textbf{fifth giant planet} was scattered off Jupiter and ejected: \emph{jumping Jupiter}.
    \item Either way: Uranus and Neptune scattered outward, the primordial Kuiper Belt disrupted, and possibly an impact spike inside (the ``Late Heavy Bombardment'', still debated).
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Tsiganis et al.\ (2005); Nesvorn\'y (2018).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the jumping-Jupiter simulation}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Panel (a)}: semi-major axes vs time for five giants in a compact resonant chain, with an extra ice giant (magenta) between Saturn and Uranus. Dashed lines mark today's values.
    \item At the instability, $\sim 6$ Myr in, the extra ice giant is scattered off Jupiter and \textbf{ejected}; Uranus and Neptune move outward onto their present orbits.
    \item \textbf{Panel (b)}: the Saturn/Jupiter period ratio starts near the 3:2 value of $1.5$, drifts up through planetesimal-driven migration, then \textbf{jumps} from $\approx 1.7$ to $\approx 2.4$ in under $10^5$ yr, approaching today's $2.49$.
    \item \textbf{The shaded band}, $2.1 \lesssim P_{\mathrm{Sat}}/P_{\mathrm{Jup}} \lesssim 2.3$: crossing it \emph{slowly} would activate secular resonances that excite the belt and the terrestrial orbits.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip1pt\relax
  \keyresult{Avoiding that band is the central argument for jumping Jupiter over the smooth 2:1 crossing; the evidence is the solar system we have.}
\end{frame}

% ── Grand Tack ──────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The Grand Tack}
  A proposal for what Jupiter did \emph{while} the gas disk was still present:
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Jupiter migrates \textbf{inward} to $\sim 1.5$ AU by Type II migration.
    \item Saturn, migrating faster, catches up and is captured into the \textbf{3:2 resonance} with Jupiter.
    \item Their combined interaction with the disk reverses the torque, and both planets move \textbf{outward}: the ``tack'', as in sailing.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip4pt\relax
  The inward excursion would have depleted solids in the inner disk, which could explain \textbf{Mars's small mass} and the belt's compositional structure.
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \keyresult{Read it as a hypothesis with consequences, not a settled result: the migration in the reference simulation is an \emph{imposed prescription}, not an outcome of the model.}
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Walsh et al.\ (2011).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the Grand Tack tracks}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Top panel}: mass growth of the four giant planets during the disk phase.
    \item \textbf{Bottom panel}: semi-major axis against time, each curve labelled with its planet.
    \item Follow Jupiter: inward to $\sim 1.5$ AU, then the \textbf{reversal} once Saturn catches up and the 3:2 resonance locks them together, then outward.
    \item \textbf{Important caveat}: these tracks are the migration \emph{prescription imposed} on the planets in the reference simulation, following the inward-then-outward path found in hydrodynamical studies. The figure shows the scenario, not a derivation of it.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ── Hot Jupiters ────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{The observational case for migration}
  The strongest evidence is a population that cannot be explained any other way: \textbf{hot Jupiters}.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Gas giants with orbital periods of a few days, $a \lesssim 0.1$ AU.
    \item They \textbf{cannot have formed in situ}: there is not enough material that close in, and it is far too hot for solids.
    \item So they formed further out and moved inward, by \emph{either}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{disk migration}, smooth and early, or
        \item \textbf{high-eccentricity migration} followed by tidal circularisation, violent and late.
      \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  Independently: the gaps ALMA sees in disks such as HL Tau may be the signature of embedded planets already migrating. We return to exoplanet demographics in L13.
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Dawson \& Johnson (2018).}
\end{frame}

\section{Recent advances}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ── ALMA transformation ─────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{What high-resolution imaging changed}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item DSHARP found rings and gaps in \textbf{all 20} of its large, bright target disks, including around stars younger than $\sim 1$ Myr.
    \item If embedded planets carve those gaps, then planet formation \textbf{begins earlier and proceeds faster} than the classical picture allowed, which is what the pebble-accretion paradigm predicts.
    \item The honest caveat: ice lines and magnetically structured zones remain viable alternatives for some of the observed structures.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \keyresult{The debate is no longer whether disks are structured. It is what the structure means.}
  \vskip2pt\relax

\end{frame}

% ── Theory synthesis ────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Where the theory stands}
  The \emph{Protostars and Planets VII} volume consolidates the current synthesis, incorporating the ALMA constraints. Key developments:
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Refined models of the \textbf{streaming instability} and the conditions under which planetesimals actually form.
    \item Improved treatment of \textbf{pebble accretion} in evolving, structured disks.
    \item New constraints on giant-planet formation timescales from \textbf{meteorite isotopes}: the inner and outer solar system stayed chemically separated early, which dates Jupiter's core.
    \item Juno gravity data revealing Jupiter's \textbf{dilute core}, which is not the sharp core-envelope boundary the classical model assumed (L8).
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  Together these point to giant-planet cores growing within the first few Myr, before the gas disperses, although \emph{how early} Jupiter's core in particular had to form is still contested (L12, L14).
  \vskip2pt\relax
  {\footnotesize Dr\k{a}\.zkowska et al.\ (2023); Kruijer et al.\ (2017); Wahl et al.\ (2017).}
\end{frame}

\section{Three questions to take forward}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ── Q1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Q1. How does nature build a planet out of dust?}
  Not by patient sticking alone.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Collisional growth stalls at the \textbf{bouncing, fragmentation, and radial-drift barriers}.
    \item The leap from pebbles to planetesimals needs the \textbf{collective physics} of the streaming instability.
    \item From there, gravitational focusing, pebble accretion, and gas capture assemble planets across \textbf{twelve orders of magnitude} in size.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \textbf{Still genuinely open:} where and when the first planetesimals formed, and how early Jupiter's core grew (L12, L14).
\end{frame}

% ── Q2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Q2. Why does an orbit's energy depend only on its semi-major axis?}
  The vis-viva equation compresses all of Keplerian dynamics into one statement:
  \vskip4pt\relax
  \begin{center}
    $a$ fixes the \textbf{energy}; \quad $e$ merely \textbf{redistributes} speed along the orbit.
  \end{center}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item From Halley's $54.5$ km s$^{-1}$ plunge at perihelion to its $0.91$ km s$^{-1}$ crawl at aphelion: same energy, same $a$, all of it eccentricity.
    \item Kepler's third law turns any measured period into a mass.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  These two tools carry directly into the exoplanet detection methods of L13, where a period and a velocity are often \emph{all} you get.
\end{frame}

% ── Q3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Q3. Why is Io the most volcanically active body in the solar system?}
  Because orbital dynamics has \textbf{thermal} consequences.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The Laplace resonance forces an eccentricity of just $0.004$.
    \item Tidal flexing converts that into more heat per kilogram than radioactivity provides anywhere else.
    \item A locked moon on a circular orbit would be geologically dead. A \textbf{resonance keeps Io molten}.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip8pt\relax
  \keyresult{Orbits are not just where a body is. They are part of its energy budget, and therefore part of its geology.}
\end{frame}

% ── Bridge to L3 ────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Next time: Lecture 3}
  This lecture assembled the planets and set their orbits. The next asks what happens \emph{inside} them.
  \vskip6pt\relax
  \textbf{L3 follows the energy:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Heat delivered by accretion and differentiation.
    \item Short-lived radionuclides, led by $^{26}\mathrm{Al}$ (half-life $0.72$ Myr), that melted even small planetesimals.
    \item Long-lived isotopes still powering Earth's $47$ TW heat flow today.
    \item Tidal dissipation, whose consequences we just saw on Io.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip6pt\relax
  Then: how that heat \emph{escapes}, by conduction and by convection. The balance between the two decides whether a planet convects, sustains a dynamo, and stays geologically alive.
\end{frame}

% ── Summary ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}{Summary}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Disks are inevitable}: angular momentum forbids direct collapse onto the star, and the disk's temperature structure sets the snow line at $2.5$--$3.5$ AU.
    \item \textbf{Growth is not incremental}: bouncing, fragmentation, and radial drift stop sticking well short of planetesimals; the streaming instability jumps the gap.
    \item \textbf{Giant-planet formation is a race} against a $\sim 3$ Myr disk lifetime, and core accretion only just wins it.
    \item \textbf{Vis-viva}, $v^2 = GM(2/r - 1/a)$, gives the speed anywhere on any orbit; the energy depends only on $a$.
    \item \textbf{Resonances and tides} convert orbital configuration into interior heat: Io's $e \approx 0.004$ is the reason it is the most volcanically active body we know.
    \item \textbf{Migration is the rule}: hot Jupiters, the Nice model, and the Grand Tack all say that where a planet is now is not where it formed.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
