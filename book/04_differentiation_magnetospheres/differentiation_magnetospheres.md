## Accretion and early melting

Gravitational energy released during accretion and differentiation melts an Earth-mass body, producing a **magma ocean** (a global layer of molten rock) ({ref}`Lecture 3 <lecture03>`).
This melting drives chemical differentiation, separating a homogeneous mixture of rock and metal into a core, mantle, and crust.

While the mantle is liquid, dense iron droplets sink at metres per second (Eq. {eq}`eq:stokes-settling` below), forming a core within weeks.
In solid rock, a viscosity of about $10^{21}$ Pa s prevents iron from sinking within the age of the universe.
Core formation must therefore happen while the planet is molten.
Chondritic asteroids survive undifferentiated to the present day because they never melted, so they never formed cores.

### Metal-silicate separation

Accretional heating and ${}^{26}\mathrm{Al}$ decay ({ref}`Lecture 3 <lecture03>`) produce magma oceans, where iron sinks to form a core {cite:p}`Lichtenberg2023`.
Droplet settling follows **Stokes' law** for a falling sphere in a viscous fluid:

$$
v_{\mathrm{Stokes}} = \frac{2}{9} \frac{\Delta\rho \, g \, r^2}{\mu}
$$ (eq:stokes-settling)

where $\Delta\rho \approx 4000$ kg m$^{-3}$ is density contrast, $g$ is gravity, $r$ is radius, and $\mu$ is viscosity.
Stokes' law holds only while the flow around the droplet stays laminar, which requires droplet radii below roughly a millimetre; centimetre-sized droplets settle turbulently at roughly $0.5$ m s$^{-1}$ instead {cite:p}`Rubie2003`.
Either way, droplets cross a magma ocean thousands of kilometres deep within days to weeks {cite:p}`Rubie2015`.

### Giant impacts and mantle stripping

For oblique, high-velocity collisions, a giant impact can remove more silicate mantle than it delivers.
This **mantle stripping** leaves a remnant with an anomalously large metallic core relative to its silicate mantle.
It explains Mercury's high iron fraction and provides a model for Mars's hemispheric dichotomy.

Mercury has a metallic core mass fraction of $\sim 74\%$ {cite:p}`MargotHauck2018` (see {ref}`Lecture 8 <lecture08>` and {ref}`Lecture 10 <lecture10>`), compared to $\sim 32\%$ for Earth and $\sim 24\%$ for Mars.
Condensation chemistry alone cannot produce this high iron fraction.
The leading hypothesis, going back to {cite:t}`Benz1988` and updated with smoothed-particle hydrodynamics simulations by {cite:t}`Benz2007` and {cite:t}`Chau2018`, is a **mantle-stripping giant impact**: an oblique collision that removed most of the silicate mantle into heliocentric orbit while leaving the metallic core intact ({numref}`fig:chau2018-mercury-impact`).

The **hemispheric dichotomy** of Mars ({ref}`Lecture 10 <lecture10>`) is the $\sim 6$ km elevation and $\sim 30$ km crustal-thickness difference between the northern lowlands and southern highlands ({numref}`fig:mola-mars-topography`).
{cite:t}`AndrewsHanna2008` and {cite:t}`Marinova2008` proposed that a giant impact excavated the northern hemisphere to produce this dichotomy.
Unlike the Mercury collision, this impact redistributed crust laterally rather than stripping mantle mass to space.

The Moon-forming impact represents the opposite regime of net accretion, where a Mars-sized body struck Earth and contributed the material that assembled the Moon.
Mercury and Earth represent the endpoints from net mantle stripping to net accretion, while Mars is an intermediate case that redistributed crust without stripping bulk mantle.

Mantle stripping also explains why Mercury's surface is depleted in iron and sulfur relative to **chondrites**, primitive meteorites that never melted and preserve solar-nebula composition.
The required impact velocities also constrain giant-planet migration during terrestrial-planet assembly ({ref}`Lecture 2 <lecture02>`).

## Core formation

The chemical consequences of core formation are profound: the separation of metal from silicate **partitions** the elements between the core and mantle (an element is said to *partition* between two coexisting phases when it distributes itself between them in a concentration ratio set by chemical equilibrium), according to their geochemical affinities.

### Goldschmidt classification

Elements are classified into four geochemical categories based on phase affinity:

| Classification | Affinity | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Siderophile** ("iron-loving") | Metallic phase (core) | Fe, Ni |
| **Lithophile** ("rock-loving") | Silicate phase (mantle) | Si, Mg |
| **Chalcophile** ("sulfur-loving") | Sulfide phase | Cu, S |
| **Atmophile** ("atmosphere-loving") | Gas phase | H, N |

This classification ({numref}`fig:goldschmidt`) depends on pressure, temperature, and **oxygen fugacity** (an effective oxygen partial pressure measuring how oxidising or reducing a melt is) during metal-silicate equilibration.
At high pressures, silicon becomes siderophile and enters the core, contributing to its density deficit relative to pure iron {cite:p}`Rubie2015, Shahar2026`.

### Partition coefficients

The degree to which an element partitions into metal is quantified by the **metal-silicate partition coefficient**:

$$
D_i^{\mathrm{met/sil}} = \frac{C_i^{\mathrm{metal}}}{C_i^{\mathrm{silicate}}}
$$ (eq:partition-coefficient)

Here $C_i^{\mathrm{metal}}$ and $C_i^{\mathrm{silicate}}$ are element concentrations in the metallic and silicate phases.
Strongly siderophile elements have $D^{\mathrm{met/sil}} \sim 10^4$ to $10^6$, concentrating overwhelmingly in the core.
The depletion of mantle iridium by a factor of $\sim 1000$ relative to chondrites provides direct evidence for core formation {cite:p}`Rubie2015`.

However, mantle siderophile elements sit near $10^{-3}$ of chondritic abundance in chondritic proportions, higher than core formation predicts.
This excess is explained by a **late veneer**, a final $\sim 0.5\%$ of planetary mass accreted from chondritic material after core formation ceased ({numref}`fig:hf-w-chronometry`) {cite:p}`Walker2009`.
Smaller late veneers are inferred for Mars and the Moon.

Partition coefficients depend strongly on pressure, temperature, and oxygen fugacity ($f_{\mathrm{O_2}}$).
High pressures at the base of a deep magma ocean (40 to 60 GPa) alter partitioning, allowing laboratory experiments to constrain core-formation conditions.

### Equilibration depth and the P-T-$f_{\mathrm{O_2}}$ dependence

As metal droplets sink through a magma ocean, siderophile element partitioning between core and mantle is determined by the $(P, T, f_{\mathrm{O_2}})$ conditions at the base of the magma ocean {cite:p}`Rubie2015`.
Partition coefficients follow an empirical **Nernst-type regression**, a thermodynamic parameterisation fit to laboratory experiments:

$$
\log_{10} D_i^{\mathrm{met/sil}} = a_i + \frac{b_i}{T} + c_i \frac{P}{T} + d_i \cdot \log_{10} f_{\mathrm{O_2}}
$$ (eq:d-parameterisation)

For moderately siderophile elements such as Ni and Co, $D_i^{\mathrm{met/sil}}$ decreases with pressure from $\sim 10^4$ at 1 GPa to $\sim 10^2$ or less at 50 GPa.
Earth's mantle retains several percent of its nickel, whereas low-pressure partitioning ($D \sim 10^3$ to $10^4$) predicts near-total depletion.
Reproducing observed mantle abundances ($D \approx 24$ to $26$ for Ni and Co) requires equilibration at 40 to 60 GPa and 3000 to 4000 K, corresponding to a magma ocean $\sim 1000$ to $1500$ km deep ({numref}`fig:partitioning-pressure`) {cite:p}`Fischer2015`.

The **oxygen fugacity** $f_{\mathrm{O_2}}$ measures the chemical availability of oxygen, represented as the effective partial pressure of $\mathrm{O_2}$ in equilibrium with the melt.
In a **reducing** environment (low $f_{\mathrm{O_2}}$) iron remains metallic, while in an **oxidising** environment (high $f_{\mathrm{O_2}}$) iron oxidises to FeO and enters the silicate.
Values are quoted relative to the **iron-wüstite (IW) buffer**, $\mathrm{Fe} + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{O_2} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{FeO}$, with IW$-$2 indicating 2 orders of magnitude below reference.
During accretion, $f_{\mathrm{O_2}}$ rose from 4 to 5 log-units below IW to near IW$-$2 today.
Increasing oxygen availability oxidises more metal into the silicate, lowering $D_i^{\mathrm{met/sil}}$ and reproducing mantle abundances in a progressively oxidising magma ocean {cite:p}`Rubie2015`.

Core formation proceeds continuously during accretion rather than as an instantaneous event.
The two-stage Hf-W model age of $\sim 30$ Myr compresses core extraction into an equivalent single event, setting a lower limit on core completion {cite:p}`KleineWalker2017`.
Because accretion was protracted, Earth's core finished forming after $\sim 30$ Myr, mostly within $\sim 100$ Myr and no later than 200 Myr.
In contrast to this planetary timescale, individual metal droplets equilibrated with the magma ocean in only days ({numref}`fig:stokes-settling`).

### Hf-W chronometry: timing core formation

The **hafnium-tungsten** (Hf-W) isotopic system dates core formation via the decay of short-lived ${}^{182}\mathrm{Hf}$ to ${}^{182}\mathrm{W}$ (half-life 8.9 million years).
Hafnium is **lithophile** (stays in the mantle) while tungsten is **moderately siderophile** (extracted into the core).
Early core formation while ${}^{182}\mathrm{Hf}$ is alive produces an excess of mantle ${}^{182}\mathrm{W}$ relative to undifferentiated chondrites ({numref}`fig:hf-w-schematic`), whereas late core formation leaves no excess.

The mantle ${}^{182}\mathrm{W}$ excess, expressed as $\varepsilon^{182}\mathrm{W}$ in parts per 10,000 deviation from chondritic, records the timing of core formation.
For Earth, $\varepsilon^{182}\mathrm{W} \approx +2$ yields a two-stage model age of $\sim 30$ Myr, a lower limit for protracted accretion that was mostly complete within $\sim 100$ Myr {cite:p}`KleineWalker2017`.
For Mars, bulk-mantle $\varepsilon^{182}\mathrm{W} \approx +0.37 \pm 0.05$, estimated from enriched **shergottites** (a class of basaltic martian meteorites), indicates accretion within $\sim 10$ Myr {cite:p}`Kruijer2017Mars`.
The Moon's $\varepsilon^{182}\mathrm{W}$ is close to Earth's value, consistent with formation from giant impact debris.

Core-formation timing across the solar system is summarised in {numref}`fig:hf-w-chronometry`.
The zero point ($t = 0$) is defined by **CAIs** (calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions, the first solids to condense from the solar nebula).
Meteoritic records of the first 5 Myr are covered in {ref}`Lecture 12 <lecture12>`, while {numref}`fig:hf-w-chronometry`b compares core formation across planetesimals, Mars, Earth, and the Moon.

#### Worked example: from $\varepsilon^{182}\mathrm{W}$ to a core-formation age

In a **two-stage model**, core formation occurs instantaneously at time $t_c$ after CAIs ($t = 0$).
Metal segregation removes tungsten into the core, elevating mantle $\mathrm{Hf/W}$.
Decay of residual $^{182}\mathrm{Hf}$ produces an excess of $^{182}\mathrm{W}$ in the mantle:

$$
\varepsilon^{182}\mathrm{W}_\mathrm{BSE} = \mathcal{Q}_\mathrm{W} \cdot \left[\frac{(\mathrm{Hf}/\mathrm{W})_\mathrm{BSE}}{(\mathrm{Hf}/\mathrm{W})_\mathrm{CHUR}} - 1\right] \cdot \left(\frac{{}^{182}\mathrm{Hf}}{{}^{180}\mathrm{Hf}}\right)_{\!\!\!0} \cdot e^{-\lambda t_c}
$$ (eq:hfw-age)

where $\mathcal{Q}_\mathrm{W} \approx 10^4$ is the prefactor, $(^{182}\mathrm{Hf}/^{180}\mathrm{Hf})_0 \approx 1.02 \times 10^{-4}$ is the initial ratio, $\lambda = 7.79 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{Myr}^{-1}$ is the decay constant ($t_{1/2} = 8.9$ Myr), and **CHUR** (chondritic uniform reservoir) denotes unfractionated composition.
For Earth's bulk silicate mantle ($\mathrm{BSE}$), $(\mathrm{Hf}/\mathrm{W})_\mathrm{BSE}/(\mathrm{Hf}/\mathrm{W})_\mathrm{CHUR} \approx 18$, so the bracketed term is $\approx 17$.

Plugging in the observed Earth value $\varepsilon^{182}\mathrm{W}_\mathrm{BSE} \approx +2$:

$$
e^{-\lambda t_c} = \frac{2}{10^4 \cdot 17 \cdot 1.02 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 0.115
$$

$$
t_c = -\frac{\ln 0.115}{\lambda} \approx \frac{2.16}{0.0779\ \mathrm{Myr}^{-1}} \approx 28 \text{ Myr}
$$

For Earth, this yields an instantaneous age of $\sim 28$ Myr.
Protracted accretion makes this a lower limit, with core formation extending to $\sim 100$ Myr {cite:p}`KleineWalker2017`.
For Mars, $(\mathrm{Hf}/\mathrm{W})_\mathrm{mantle}/(\mathrm{Hf}/\mathrm{W})_\mathrm{CHUR} \approx 4$ and $\varepsilon^{182}\mathrm{W} \approx +0.4$ yield a model age of $\sim 26$ Myr.
Since martian core formation ended within $\sim 10$ Myr ({numref}`fig:hf-w-chronometry`), this signature dates magma-ocean crystallisation at $\sim 20$ to $25$ Myr {cite:p}`Kruijer2017Mars`.
Mars thus accreted and differentiated before $^{182}\mathrm{Hf}$ became extinct.

The Hf-W system only dates processes occurring while $^{182}\mathrm{Hf}$ is alive, within $\sim 45$ Myr of CAIs.
Later differentiation leaves no $\varepsilon^{182}\mathrm{W}$ signature, so events such as inner core nucleation require thermal-evolution modelling ({ref}`Lecture 8 <lecture08>`).

## Mantle differentiation

Core formation is not the end of chemical differentiation. As the magma ocean cools and crystallises, it produces further chemical layering within the silicate mantle itself.

### Magma ocean crystallisation

As a magma ocean cools, solidification begins where its **adiabat** (the temperature profile of a convecting fluid with depth) meets the melting curves of **peridotite** (mantle rock).
Below the **solidus** the mantle is solid, above the **liquidus** it is liquid, and between them crystals and melt form a **mush**.
Because melting curves rise more steeply with pressure than the adiabat, cooling crosses the liquidus first at the base of the mantle ({numref}`fig:magma-ocean-crystallisation`).
A deep magma ocean therefore crystallises from the bottom up {cite:p}`ElkinsTanton2012`, forming high-pressure **bridgmanite** ($\mathrm{MgSiO_3}$) at depth and **olivine** at shallower depths.

Solidification displaces liquid upward, enriching remaining melt in incompatible elements (U, Th, K, and rare earths).
The final stages leave a buoyant residual liquid enriched in volatiles and heat-producing elements at the top of the mantle.

At lowermost-mantle pressures, the density contrast between melt and crystals shrinks, allowing iron-rich melt to become denser than solids.
This dense melt can persist as a long-lived **basal magma ocean** {cite:p}`Labrosse2007`.
InSight seismology indicates a molten silicate layer above the core-mantle boundary of Mars {cite:p}`Samuel2023`.

### Incompatible element enrichment

During crystallisation, **incompatible elements** that do not fit into mineral crystal lattices concentrate in the residual liquid.
These include the heat-producing isotopes uranium (U), thorium (Th), and potassium (K) ({ref}`Lecture 3 <lecture03>`).

The residual liquid solidifies to form the crust.
Continental crust is enriched in U, Th, and K by a factor of $\sim$50–100 relative to the upper mantle, producing roughly half of Earth's radiogenic heat in less than 1% of Earth's mass.

### Mantle reservoirs

The crystallisation of a magma ocean does not produce a perfectly homogeneous mantle. Instead, it creates compositionally distinct **mantle reservoirs** that persist to the present day:

- **Depleted MORB mantle (DMM):** The upper mantle source of mid-ocean ridge basalts (MORBs) has been depleted in incompatible elements by billions of years of partial melting and crustal extraction. This is the most voluminous mantle reservoir.

- **Enriched mantle:** Deep mantle plumes that produce ocean island basalts (OIBs, e.g., Hawaii, Iceland) sample reservoirs that are enriched in incompatible elements, possibly representing less-processed primordial mantle material or recycled oceanic crust.

### The lunar magma ocean

The Moon provides the clearest example of magma ocean crystallisation.
Apollo 11 returned fragments of white **anorthosite** (rock made almost entirely of plagioclase feldspar) from Mare Tranquillitatis in 1969.
To explain a global plagioclase crust, {cite:t}`Wood1970` proposed that buoyant plagioclase crystals floated to the surface of a deep magma ocean.

Because of its small size and single-plate tectonic regime, the Moon has largely preserved this magma-ocean stratigraphy ({numref}`fig:lunar-magma-ocean`).
Beneath the $\sim 40$ to $50$ km anorthositic crust lies a **cumulate** mantle (rock built from crystals that settled out of the magma) grading from pyroxenite to dunite.
The last residual liquid crystallised at the base of the crust into a layer enriched in dense ilmenite and **KREEP** (potassium, rare earth elements, and phosphorus), which is thought to have sunk through the underlying mantle in an early gravitational overturn {cite:p}`ElkinsTanton2012`.

## Volatile delivery and retention

The chemical differentiation discussed so far concerns the refractory (high-temperature) components of planets. But the **volatile elements** (hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and the noble gases) are equally important, because they form the raw materials for atmospheres, oceans, and ultimately life.

### The classical view and its revision

In the classical view, terrestrial planets formed inside the **snow line** (the distance from the Sun beyond which water condenses as ice, at $\sim$2--3 AU) from dry rock.
Water and other volatiles were thought to arrive late, delivered by asteroids and comets scattered inward after the planets had finished growing ({numref}`fig:volatile-delivery`a).

Meteorite measurements demonstrate that the inner solar system was never completely dry.
All chondrite groups, including ordinary and enstatite chondrites from the inner disk, accreted water ice during their formation {cite:p}`Alexander2019b`.
Because the snow line migrated outward during peak accretional heating and then retreated inward as the disk cooled, early inner planetesimals accreted ice directly {cite:p}`Grewal2024`.

As Jupiter grew, it dammed the inward drift of icy pebbles and split the disk into two distinct planetesimal reservoirs ({numref}`fig:volatile-delivery`b).
This division produced the isotopically distinct non-carbonaceous (NC) and carbonaceous (CC) meteorite families.
Isotopic measurements of nitrogen, carbon, and sulfur indicate that Earth accreted volatiles from both reservoirs during its main growth rather than from a late veneer {cite:p}`Grewal2021`.

Although inner building blocks incorporated ice, terrestrial planets are dry today, with Earth's surface water amounting to only a few hundredths of a percent of its mass.
Growing protoplanets lost most of this water during accretion through three main mechanisms {cite:p}`Lichtenberg2023`.

First, decay heat from $^{26}$Al melted early planetesimals and drove off their ice ({ref}`Lecture 3 <lecture03>`), leaving dry, differentiated bodies {cite:p}`Lichtenberg2019`.
Second, **impact devolatilisation** (the escape of vaporised species during collisions) removed volatiles and moderately volatile elements like magnesium during giant impacts {cite:p}`Hin2017`.

Third, infalling icy pebbles ablated in primordial hydrogen envelopes, recycling water vapour into the disk rather than delivering it to the surface {cite:p}`Johansen2021`.
A planet's final volatile budget is therefore set early by the timing of planetesimal and giant planet formation, and trimmed during accretion by radiogenic heating, collisional loss, and atmospheric processing {cite:p}`Krijt2023`.

### Outgassing from the magma ocean

Much of a planet's initial volatile budget is dissolved in the silicate magma ocean.
As the magma ocean cools and crystallises, dissolved volatiles are released to form a **secondary atmosphere** through **outgassing** {cite:p}`Hirschmann2012`.

Atmospheric speciation depends on the **oxygen fugacity** of the magma:

- Under **reducing** conditions (low $f_{\mathrm{O_2}}$): dominant species are $\mathrm{H_2}$, CO, and $\mathrm{N_2}$
- Under **oxidising** conditions (high $f_{\mathrm{O_2}}$): dominant species are $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{CO_2}$, and $\mathrm{N_2}$

Volatile partitioning between melt and atmosphere is set by gas **solubility** in silicate melt.
Water dissolves as hydroxyl groups, whereas $\mathrm{CO_2}$ forms carbonate ions and is roughly two orders of magnitude less soluble.
Because dissolving water splits into two hydroxyl groups, the dissolved water fraction grows with the square root of vapour pressure:

$$
X_{\mathrm{H_2O}} \propto p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}^{1/2}
$$ (eq:water-solubility)

where $X_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$ is the mole fraction of dissolved water and $p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$ is the vapour pressure above the melt.
This feedback allows the magma ocean to retain more water in solution as the atmosphere thickens {cite:p}`Hirschmann2012`.

Most of a planet's water thus remains in the magma during the magma ocean stage ({numref}`fig:outgassing-speciation`) {cite:p}`Nicholls2024`.
Across most oxidation states, Earth's mantle melt holds $\sim 5 \times 10^{21}$ kg of water (over 3 times the ocean mass) while the atmosphere contains only a few bar of steam.
Poorly soluble carbon instead dominates the atmosphere as $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and CO at hundreds to thousands of bar.

### Impact erosion versus delivery

Giant impacts deliver volatiles but can also strip them away.
**Impact erosion** (atmospheric loss driven by collisions) operates in two distinct regimes depending on impactor size.

In **local erosion**, an impactor vaporises upon impact, driving a plume that accelerates overlying gas beyond escape velocity.
A single impact removes at most the atmosphere above the plane tangent to the surface {cite:p}`MeloshVickery1989`.
For a thin atmosphere ($H \ll R$), the tangent-plane fraction is

$$
f_\mathrm{tp} \approx \frac{H}{2R}
$$ (eq:tangent-plane-fraction)

about $6 \times 10^{-4}$ of the global inventory for Earth ($H \approx 8$ km).
Because a single impact removes little gas, local erosion operates through cumulative bombardment.
Small planetesimals are the most efficient eroders per unit mass because their energy couples directly into the atmosphere {cite:p}`Schlichting2015`.

In **global erosion**, a giant impact sends a shock through the interior that sets the ground into motion.
Wherever ground velocity satisfies

$$
v_\mathrm{ground} \gtrsim v_\mathrm{esc},
$$ (eq:ground-motion-blowoff)

the overlying atmospheric column is expelled.
In the Moon-forming impact, ground velocities remained well below Earth's $v_\mathrm{esc} \approx 11$ km s$^{-1}$, removing only $\sim 20\%$ of the atmosphere {cite:p}`GendaAbe2003`.
However, a surface ocean enhances loss because water vaporisation and lower **shock impedance** (the product of density and wave speed) transmit ground motion more efficiently {cite:p}`GendaAbe2005`.
This ocean effect may explain why dry Venus retains roughly 50 times more $^{36}\mathrm{Ar}$ than Earth.

Three-dimensional simulations resolve two distinct channels within a single event ({numref}`fig:impact-atmosphere-loss`): **near-field loss** from ejecta plumes, and **far-field loss** from global shock breakout {cite:p}`Roche2025`.
Atmospheric loss ranges from a few percent in oblique collisions near escape velocity to near-total removal in high-velocity head-on impacts.

Earth's **primordial H$_2$-He envelope** was stripped by cumulative impacts alongside solar XUV-driven thermal escape ({ref}`Lecture 5 <lecture05>`).
For Mars, with lower escape velocity ($\approx 5$ km s$^{-1}$), a Borealis-scale impact ({cite:t}`Marinova2008`) and solar-wind stripping removed most of the early atmosphere ({ref}`Lecture 10 <lecture10>`).

Planetary volatile inventories reflect the balance between impact delivery and erosion, set by impactor size, velocity, and volatile content {cite:p}`Schlichting2015`.
Earth's volatile budget combines this balance with secondary outgassing from the magma ocean.

The evolution of outgassed atmospheres is discussed in {ref}`Lecture 5 <lecture05>` and {ref}`Lecture 6 <lecture06>`.

## Planetary magnetic fields and dynamo theory

The differentiation of a planet into a metallic core and silicate mantle sets the stage for one of the most important geophysical phenomena: the generation of a planetary **magnetic field** by dynamo action in the liquid metallic core.

### Why magnetic fields matter

A global magnetic field deflects charged solar wind particles, reducing surface radiation and atmospheric erosion.
This limits **sputtering** (atmospheric atoms knocked loose by energetic particles) and **ion pickup** (ionised particles swept away by the solar wind).
Mars lost much of its atmosphere after losing its global field $\sim$4 Gyr ago ({ref}`Lecture 10 <lecture10>`).

Magnetic minerals record the ambient field when cooling through their **Curie temperature** (the temperature above which a mineral loses its permanent magnetism).
The field also provides direct constraints on the physical state, composition, and dynamics of a planet's deep interior.

### The induction equation

The **magnetic induction equation** describes magnetic field evolution in a conducting fluid:

$$
\pdv{\vec{B}}{t} = \nabla \times (\vec{v} \times \vec{B}) + \eta \nabla^2 \vec{B}
$$ (eq:induction-equation)

where $\vec{B}$ is magnetic field, $\vec{v}$ is fluid velocity, and $\eta$ is **magnetic diffusivity**:

$$
\eta = \frac{1}{\mu_0 \sigma}
$$ (eq:magnetic-diffusivity)

with permeability $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ H m$^{-1}$ and electrical conductivity $\sigma$.

Two competing terms govern field survival {cite:p}`Roberts2013`.
Advection $\nabla \times (\vec{v} \times \vec{B})$ stretches field lines to amplify magnetic flux.
Diffusion $\eta \nabla^2 \vec{B}$ causes ohmic decay that destroys flux.

### Requirements for dynamo action

A **self-sustaining dynamo** generates and maintains a magnetic field against ohmic decay when three conditions are met:

1. An electrically conducting fluid to carry currents (liquid iron in terrestrial planets, metallic hydrogen in gas giants).
2. Convection driven by thermal or compositional buoyancy.
3. Flow vigour where advection dominates diffusion, requiring the **magnetic Reynolds number** $\mathrm{Rm}$ to exceed $\mathrm{Rm}_c \sim 10$ to $100$.

Convective columns aligned with the rotation axis stretch and twist field lines to regenerate the field ({numref}`fig:geodynamo-schematic`).

## Blackboard derivation: The magnetic Reynolds number

```{admonition} Blackboard derivation: The magnetic Reynolds number $\mathrm{Rm}$
:class: tip

**Goal:** Derive the magnetic Reynolds number $\mathrm{Rm} = UL/\eta$ from the induction equation by dimensional analysis, and estimate $\mathrm{Rm}$ for Earth's outer core to demonstrate that advection dominates over diffusion, the fundamental criterion for dynamo action.

**Setup.**

We start from the magnetic induction equation introduced in the previous section (Eq. {eq}`eq:induction-equation`):

$$
\pdv{\vec{B}}{t} = \underbrace{\nabla \times (\vec{v} \times \vec{B})}_{\text{advection}} + \underbrace{\eta \nabla^2 \vec{B}}_{\text{diffusion}}
$$

We want to determine which term dominates for a given flow. If advection wins, the flow can amplify magnetic field; if diffusion wins, any field decays away.

**Derivation.**

We use **dimensional analysis** to estimate the magnitude of each term. Let $U$ be a characteristic flow velocity, $L$ a characteristic length scale, and $B$ the field strength.

**Advection term:**

$$
|\nabla \times (\vec{v} \times \vec{B})| \sim \frac{UB}{L}
$$

since the curl introduces a factor of $1/L$ and the cross product introduces a factor of $UB$.

**Diffusion term:**

$$
|\eta \nabla^2 \vec{B}| \sim \frac{\eta B}{L^2}
$$

since each spatial derivative introduces a factor of $1/L$, and $\nabla^2$ involves two spatial derivatives.

The ratio of the advection term to the diffusion term defines the **magnetic Reynolds number**:

$$
\mathrm{Rm} = \frac{|{\text{advection}}|}{|{\text{diffusion}}|} = \frac{UB/L}{\eta B/L^2}
$$

Notice that the magnetic field strength $B$ **cancels**: this is an important result. The magnetic Reynolds number is a property of the **flow**, not of the field:

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Rm} = \frac{UL}{\eta}}
$$ (eq:magnetic-reynolds)

When $\mathrm{Rm} \gg 1$, advection dominates: the magnetic field is "frozen in" to the fluid and carried along with the flow. When $\mathrm{Rm} \ll 1$, diffusion dominates: the field decays regardless of the flow.

For a self-sustaining dynamo, numerical simulations and theory indicate that the critical value is $\mathrm{Rm}_c \sim 10$–$100$, depending on the flow geometry.

**Application: Earth's outer core.**

For Earth's outer core, the relevant parameters are {cite:p}`Schubert2001`:

| Parameter | Symbol | Value |
|-----------|:------:|:-----:|
| Outer core radius | $L$ | $\sim 3.5 \times 10^6$ m |
| Typical flow velocity | $U$ | $\sim 5 \times 10^{-4}$ m s$^{-1}$ |
| Magnetic diffusivity | $\eta$ | $\sim 1$ m$^2$ s$^{-1}$ |

This gives:

$$
\mathrm{Rm}_\oplus = \frac{UL}{\eta} = \frac{5 \times 10^{-4} \times 3.5 \times 10^6}{1} \approx 1750
$$

Since $\mathrm{Rm}_\oplus \approx 1750 \gg \mathrm{Rm}_c$, advection overwhelmingly dominates over diffusion in Earth's outer core. The flow is vigorous enough to sustain dynamo action.

We can also estimate the **ohmic diffusion timescale**, how long the field would take to decay if the flow suddenly stopped:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{ohm}} \sim \frac{L^2}{\eta} = \frac{(3.5 \times 10^6)^2}{1} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{13} \text{ s} \approx 400{,}000 \text{ yr}
$$

This is much less than Earth's age (4.5 Gyr), confirming that the field cannot be a relic; it must be **continuously regenerated** by dynamo action.

**Note:** Paleomagnetic measurements of ancient rocks show that Earth has had a magnetic field for at least **3.4–3.5 billion years** {cite:p}`Tarduno2010`. This places a strong constraint on the thermal and compositional evolution of the core: the convective driving mechanism must have been sustained over most of Earth's history.
```

### Driving mechanisms

Outer core convection is driven by two buoyancy sources {cite:p}`Roberts2013`.
**Thermal convection** operates when mantle cooling sinks dense fluid, provided heat loss exceeds adiabatic conduction.
**Compositional convection** occurs as inner core crystallisation rejects light elements (S, Si, O).

Inner core growth at about 0.5 mm per year makes compositional buoyancy the dominant geodynamo driver ({numref}`fig:core-convection-drivers`).

### Field morphology

Earth's magnetic field at the surface is predominantly **dipolar** (resembling the field of a bar magnet), with the dipole axis tilted approximately 11° from the rotation axis. The surface field strength ranges from about 25 $\mu$T near the equator to about 65 $\mu$T near the poles, with a mean of about 45 $\mu$T.

### Secular variation

**Secular variation** is the change in the magnetic field on decadal to century timescales, tracing turbulent outer-core flow.
Non-dipole components drift, grow, and decay, with many features exhibiting **westward drift** at roughly 0.2° per year from differential core-mantle rotation.
The **South Atlantic Anomaly** is a region where the field is about 30% weaker than the global average at that latitude, caused by a reversed-flux patch at the core-mantle boundary.

### Polarity reversals

The geomagnetic field undergoes **polarity reversals**, in which the north and south magnetic poles swap.
A reversal takes roughly 1000 to 10,000 years, averaging 4 to 5 reversals per million years.

**Magnetic stripes** are symmetric patterns of magnetised crust formed at mid-ocean ridges, recording alternating field directions ({numref}`fig:geomagnetic-polarity`).

### The dynamo scaling law

The **dynamo scaling law** relates surface dipole field $B_\mathrm{dip}$ to convective buoyancy flux $F_q$ and shell thickness $D$ {cite:p}`ChristensenAubert2006`:

$$
B_\mathrm{dip} \propto \sqrt{\rho_\mathrm{core} \mu_0} \cdot \big( F_q \cdot D \big)^{1/3}
$$ (eq:dynamo-scaling)

where $\rho_\mathrm{core}$ is fluid density and $\mu_0$ is free-space permeability.
In rapidly rotating cores, convective energy generation sets field strength ({numref}`fig:dynamo-scaling`).

### Mercury

Mercury possesses a weak but **active** dynamo: its surface field is only about 1% of Earth's, and the MESSENGER mission revealed that it is strongly offset northward from the planet's centre {cite:p}`Anderson2012`. The liquid part of Mercury's large core is thought to form only a thin shell, perhaps a few hundred kilometres thick, above a solidifying interior; in the favoured iron-snow scenario, iron crystallises near the top of this shell and the sinking crystals release compositional buoyancy only episodically. The product $F_q \cdot D$ in Eq. {eq}`eq:dynamo-scaling` is therefore small, and Mercury's dynamo is intrinsically weak at its source, not merely attenuated on the way to the surface.

### Venus

Venus is nearly identical to Earth in size and bulk composition, yet has no detected magnetic field.
Slow rotation alone cannot explain this, because dynamo simulations show that even a 243-day rotation period leaves the core rotation-dominated.
The leading explanation combines two factors: no solid inner core has nucleated to drive compositional convection, and a stagnant mantle lid ({ref}`Lecture 9 <lecture09>`) extracts heat too slowly for thermal convection.
In Eq. {eq}`eq:dynamo-scaling`, both $F_q$ and the convecting-shell geometry are suppressed.
Venus is thus probably limited not by core composition, but by mantle dynamics controlling core heat loss {cite:p}`dePaterLissauer2010`.

### Mars

Mars has no global magnetic field today, but preserves **remnant crustal magnetism** (patches of crustal magnetisation) in the ancient southern highlands ({numref}`fig:mars-crustal-magnetism`) {cite:p}`Acuna1999`.
The dynamo shut down between $\sim 4.1$ Ga {cite:p}`Acuna1999` and $\sim 3.7$ Ga {cite:p}`Mittelholz2020`, leaving the atmosphere unshielded against solar wind erosion ({ref}`Lecture 10 <lecture10>`).

### Jupiter

Convection in **metallic hydrogen** (an electrical conductor at pressures above $\sim$100 GPa) generates Jupiter's asymmetric magnetic field ({numref}`fig:jupiter-great-blue-spot`) {cite:p}`Connerney2022`.

```{figure} figures/jupiter_great_blue_spot.avif

### Ganymede

Ganymede ($R = 2634$ km) is the only moon with an **intrinsic dynamo**, generated in a liquid iron shell around a crystallising inner core {cite:p}`dePaterLissauer2010`.
Because its core is small ($R_\mathrm{core} \sim 700$ km) and deep, the surface retains only $(R_\mathrm{core}/R_\mathrm{body})^3 \approx 2\%$ of the dynamo-region field.
Yet Ganymede's surface field ($\sim 0.7$ $\mu$T) exceeds Mercury's ($\sim 0.3$ $\mu$T), which points to a larger $F_q \cdot D$ product in Eq. {eq}`eq:dynamo-scaling` sustained by steadier compositional buoyancy than Mercury's episodic iron snow.

## Magnetosphere-solar wind interaction

A planetary magnetic field does not exist in isolation: it interacts dynamically with the **solar wind**, the continuous stream of charged particles (mostly protons and electrons) flowing outward from the Sun at speeds of 300–800 km s$^{-1}$, carrying an embedded magnetic field (the interplanetary magnetic field, or IMF) {cite:p}`Kivelson1995`.

### Magnetopause and magnetosphere structure

The **magnetopause** is the boundary where solar wind ram pressure balances planetary magnetic pressure:

$$
\frac{1}{2} \rho_{\mathrm{sw}} v_{\mathrm{sw}}^2 = \frac{B_{\mathrm{mp}}^2}{2\mu_0}
$$ (eq:magnetopause-pressure)

where $\rho_{\mathrm{sw}}$ and $v_{\mathrm{sw}}$ are solar wind density and velocity, and $B_{\mathrm{mp}}$ is magnetic field strength.
For a dipole field ($B \propto r^{-3}$), this sets the **standoff distance**:

$$
r_{\mathrm{mp}} \approx 10 \, R_\oplus
$$ (eq:magnetopause-distance)

for Earth under typical solar wind conditions ({numref}`fig:magnetopause-balance`).

### Van Allen radiation belts

The **Van Allen radiation belts** are two toroidal regions where Earth's magnetosphere traps energetic charged particles ({numref}`fig:van-allen-belts`) {cite:p}`dePaterLissauer2010`.
These trapped particles pose hazards to spacecraft and astronauts, showing that magnetic fields shield the surface while trapping energetic radiation.

```{figure} figures/van_allen_belts.svg
:name: fig:van-allen-belts
:width: 550px
:align: center

The inner belt of energetic protons (10–100 MeV) is centred at $\sim 1.5\,R_\oplus$ and is largely stable; the outer belt of energetic electrons (0.1–10 MeV) is centred at $\sim 4$–$5\,R_\oplus$ and is highly variable, expanding and contracting in response to geomagnetic storms. ## Recent advances

NASA's Juno mission has produced the most detailed map of Jupiter's magnetic field to date {cite:p}`Connerney2022`. The data reveal a surprisingly complex field morphology: in the northern hemisphere, the field shows strong non-dipolar features, including a localised patch of reversed polarity (the "Great Blue Spot") that may reflect the dynamics of a deep-seated dynamo operating in the metallic hydrogen layer ({ref}`Lecture 8 <lecture08>`). These measurements provide direct constraints on the depth and nature of convection driving Jupiter's dynamo.

### InSight: no present-day Mars dynamo

NASA's InSight lander detected no global dynamo field on Mars {cite:p}`Johnson2020`.
Seismic data indicate a fully liquid core ({numref}`fig:stahler2021-mars-core`) {cite:p}`Stahler2021`.
Without compositional buoyancy from inner-core growth, thermal convection ceased once initial heat was lost ({ref}`Lecture 10 <lecture10>`).

### The lunar dynamo paradox

Paleomagnetic measurements of Apollo samples have established that the Moon itself once hosted a core dynamo. The remanent magnetisation of lunar rocks returned by Apollo 15, 16, and 17 implies paleofield strengths of tens of microtesla in the high-field epoch from $\sim 3.85$ to $\sim 3.56$ Ga (mean $\sim 77\,\mu$T), dropping below $\sim 7\,\mu$T by $\sim 3.3$ Ga {cite:p}`Weiss2014`. A microtesla-scale field at a body with only $\sim 400$ km liquid core radius is difficult to generate by purely thermal convection; proposed mechanisms include differential rotation of the core driven by tidal or impact-induced torques, and compositional buoyancy from core crystallisation similar to the scenarios proposed for Mercury and Ganymede ({numref}`fig:dynamo-scaling`).

### The Earth inner-core age debate

Earth's inner core is widely thought to have nucleated within the past $\sim 1$ Gyr.
High iron thermal conductivity from first-principles calculations yields an inner-core age of only $\sim 0.5$ to $1$ Gyr {cite:p}`Labrosse2015`.
Paleomagnetic records show intensified dipole field strength $\sim 0.5$ to $1.0$ Gyr ago, interpreted as the onset of compositional convection during **inner-core nucleation** (solid iron freezing at the core center) {cite:p}`Nimmo2015`.
These independent lines of evidence place nucleation near 540 million years ago (0.54 Ga).

Before nucleation, the **geodynamo** (the mechanism generating Earth's magnetic field) was purely thermally driven.
The survival of the field for $\sim 3$ billion years in that regime requires higher core heat flows than compositional convection demands.

### BepiColombo, JUICE, and Europa Clipper

The ESA/JAXA BepiColombo mission (orbital insertion late $2026$) provides measurements of Mercury's weak dipolar magnetic field.
Operating two orbiters simultaneously will correlate in-situ magnetospheric measurements with surface and interior constraints {cite:p}`Benkhoff2021`.

ESA's JUICE mission will investigate Ganymede, the only moon in the solar system known to generate its own dynamo.
How a body as small as Ganymede ($R = 2634$ km) maintains an active dynamo constrains the thermal state and composition of its iron core.

NASA's Europa Clipper (launched 2024, arriving at Jupiter in 2030) carries a magnetometer to measure the induced magnetic signature of Europa's subsurface ocean.
Measuring this induced response to Jupiter's ambient field constrains ocean salinity, ocean thickness, and core thermal state ({ref}`Lecture 8 <lecture08>`).
This provides magnetic-field remote sensing of Europa's interior, with habitability implications explored in {ref}`Lecture 14 <lecture14>`.
