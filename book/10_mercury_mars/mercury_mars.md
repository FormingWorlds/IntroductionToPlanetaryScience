## Part 1: Mercury — the metal world

### Mercury overview: the smallest, densest, closest

- Mass 0.055 $\Mearth$, radius 0.383 $\Rearth$, mean density 5.43 g/cm$^3$
- Second highest mean density in the solar system after Earth; **highest uncompressed density**
- Orbit: $a = 0.39$ AU, $e = 0.206$ (most eccentric among planets), $i = 7$°
- Preview of the "why so dense?" problem $\rightarrow$ giant impact vs.\ evaporation hypotheses

### Orbit and spin-orbit resonance

- Orbital period: 88 Earth days
- Sidereal rotation period: 59 Earth days
- **3:2 spin-orbit resonance**: three rotations per two orbits; a solar day on Mercury lasts $\sim$176 Earth days
- Tidal evolution drove Mercury into 3:2 rather than 1:1 because of orbital eccentricity and the planet's permanent quadrupole moment (Correia \& Laskar 2004)
- Consequence: fixed "hot poles" and "warm poles" — surface locations that alternately face the Sun at perihelion over the Mercury year

### Mercury's interior

- **Core mass fraction $\sim$70%** (vs.\ Earth's $\sim$32%) — unique in the solar system
- Liquid outer core + solid inner core (confirmed by MESSENGER libration measurements, Margot et al.\ 2007)
- Thin mantle ($\sim$400 km); thin crust ($\sim$35 km average)
- $C/MR^2 \approx 0.346$ (recap from {ref}`lecture08`) $\rightarrow$ strongly differentiated
- Weak active dynamo ($\sim$1% of Earth's dipole moment)
- **Field offset $\sim$480 km northward** from the geometric centre — unique in the solar system
- Thin liquid layer limits convective vigour; scaling laws ({ref}`lecture04`) consistent with the weak field

### Mercury's surface morphology

- Heavily cratered ancient terrain, similar in appearance to the lunar highlands
- **Smooth plains**: younger volcanic resurfacing, $\sim$3.7--3.9 Ga, analogous to lunar maria
- **Lobate scarps**: thrust faults 1--3 km high and hundreds of km long, recording global contraction as the core cooled; planet radius shrank by $\sim$7 km over its history
- **Hollows**: small, bright, flat-floored depressions unique to Mercury; likely caused by volatile loss from the crust (MESSENGER discovery, Blewett et al.\ 2011)
- **Caloris Basin**: $\sim$1550 km diameter, one of the largest impact basins in the solar system; antipodal chaotic terrain from seismic focusing

### Mercury's polar volatiles

- Permanently shadowed craters at the poles host **water ice deposits**
- Radar-bright signatures first detected by Arecibo (1991)
- Confirmed by MESSENGER neutron spectroscopy and direct imaging of exposed ice
- Source: cometary and asteroidal delivery; volatiles cold-trap on billion-year timescales
- Persistence requires near-zero obliquity (Mercury's 2° axial tilt) so that shadowed floors never see the Sun
- **Paradox**: the hottest planet's dayside hosts some of the coldest locations in the inner solar system ($\sim$100 K in permanent shadow)

### Mercury's exosphere and magnetosphere

- No true atmosphere; a collisionless **exosphere** of Na, K, Ca, Mg, H, He
- Sources: sputtering by the solar wind, micrometeorite impact vaporisation, photon-stimulated desorption
- **Sodium tail**: visible from Earth-based telescopes, extends millions of km antisunward
- Compact magnetosphere: standoff distance only $\sim$1.5 $R_{\mathrm{Mercury}}$
- Induced currents in the liquid core contribute to the observed field configuration
- Reconnection rate $\sim$10$\times$ Earth's (relative to field strength) — a very dynamic space environment

### Mission history at Mercury

- **Mariner 10** (1974--75): three flybys, $\sim$45% of the surface imaged
- **MESSENGER** (2011--2015): first orbiter; global mapping, composition, topography, field; discovery of hollows and polar ice
- **BepiColombo** (ESA/JAXA, 2018--): multiple flybys 2021--2024 en route to orbit insertion in 2026
  - Deploys two orbiters at Mercury: MPO (Mercury Planetary Orbiter) and MMO (Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter)
  - Key open questions: core composition, polar ice inventory, magnetosphere variability


## Part 2: Mars — the watery past

### Mars overview: half Earth, tenth the mass

- Mass 0.107 $\Mearth$, radius 0.532 $\Rearth$, mean density 3.93 g/cm$^3$ (lowest among the rocky planets)
- Orbit: $a = 1.52$ AU, $e = 0.093$, obliquity 25° (similar to Earth's)
- Thin $\mathrm{CO_2}$ atmosphere ($\sim$6 mbar), surface $T \approx 210$ K average
- Two small moons: Phobos and Deimos (dedicated slide below)

### Phobos and Deimos

- **Phobos**: radius $\sim$11 km, orbital period 7.6 hr (faster than Mars' rotation); orbital decay $\rightarrow$ will crash into Mars or tidally disrupt in $\sim$30--50 Myr
- **Deimos**: radius $\sim$6 km, orbital period 30 hr; slowly receding
- Both irregularly shaped, C-type spectroscopy, low density $\rightarrow$ high porosity, regolith-covered
- **Origin debate**:
  - Captured asteroids (favoured by low density and spectrum)
  - Giant-impact debris (favoured by near-equatorial, near-circular orbits)
- **JAXA MMX mission** (Martian Moons eXploration, launch 2026, return 2031):
  - Will land on Phobos and collect $\geq$10 g of regolith
  - First sample return from the Mars system
  - Expected to definitively settle the origin question

### Mars interior

- Core radius $1830 \pm 40$ km ($\sim$54% of planet radius) — larger than pre-InSight models
- **Liquid outer core** (confirmed by S-wave shadow in InSight marsquake data)
- Light-element-rich core (S, O) explains the low bulk density
- No inner core solidification $\rightarrow$ no present-day dynamo
- Crustal thickness 24--72 km (thin northern lowlands, thick southern highlands)
- $C/MR^2 \approx 0.364$ (recap from {ref}`lecture08`)

### Mars geological epochs

- **Noachian** ($>$3.7 Ga): heavy bombardment, widespread valley networks, clay mineral formation, active dynamo
- **Hesperian** ($\sim$3.7--3.0 Ga): massive outflow channels, large volcanic provinces, sulfate-rich acidic conditions
- **Amazonian** (3.0 Ga--present): cold, dry, sporadic volcanism, thin atmosphere
- Boundaries set by crater counting calibrated to the Neukum lunar chronology
- Each epoch has characteristic mineral assemblages: phyllosilicates $\rightarrow$ sulfates $\rightarrow$ anhydrous ferric oxides (Bibring et al.\ 2006)

### Mars surface highlights

- **Hemispheric dichotomy**: $\sim$6 km elevation difference between southern highlands (ancient, cratered) and northern lowlands (young, smooth); likely from a giant impact early in Mars' history
- **Olympus Mons**: largest volcano in the solar system, 21.9 km tall, 600 km diameter; a shield volcano built over a long-lived stationary mantle plume (no plate tectonics to migrate the volcano away)
- **Tharsis Bulge**: continent-sized volcanic province elevating $\sim$1/4 of the planet's surface; stress from the bulge cracked the crust and formed Valles Marineris
- **Valles Marineris**: 4000 km long, up to 200 km wide, 7 km deep — the largest canyon system in the solar system; a tectonic rift, not a fluvial canyon
- **Hellas Basin**: 2300 km diameter, $\sim$8 km deep, ancient impact basin
- **Polar caps**: seasonal $\mathrm{CO_2}$ ice + permanent water ice; dramatic seasonal exchange of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ between caps and atmosphere

### Evidence for past water

- **Valley networks**: dendritic drainage systems resembling terrestrial rivers, dominantly Noachian
- **Outflow channels**: catastrophic flood features (e.g., Kasei Valles), discharges orders of magnitude larger than any terrestrial river; Hesperian
- **Clay minerals (phyllosilicates)**: formed by low-temperature aqueous alteration; mapped by OMEGA (Mars Express) and CRISM (MRO)
- **Sulfate deposits**: later, more acidic water; Opportunity at Meridiani Planum, Curiosity at Gale crater
- **Deltas and lakebeds**: Jezero crater (Perseverance), Gale crater (Curiosity), Eberswalde
- **Jökulhlaup-style outburst features**: catastrophic release of subsurface ice reservoirs
- Crater-density constraints: Noachian valley networks $\rightarrow$ warm/wet conditions episodic or sustained; Hesperian $\rightarrow$ acidic transition; Amazonian $\rightarrow$ cold and dry

### The early Mars climate puzzle

- Faint young Sun: early Mars received only $\sim$30% of modern solar flux
- Yet valley networks and open-basin lakes require sustained liquid water
- Pure $\mathrm{CO_2}$ atmosphere is insufficient (Wordsworth 2016): $\mathrm{CO_2}$ condenses into ice clouds at high pressure, increasing albedo and cooling the planet further
- Current best ideas:
  - Reducing greenhouse gases ($\mathrm{H_2}$, $\mathrm{CH_4}$) from volcanism and serpentinisation
  - Collisionally induced absorption by $\mathrm{H_2}$-$\mathrm{CO_2}$ (Wordsworth et al.\ 2017, 2021)
  - Episodic warm intervals driven by large impacts or volcanic outbursts
- Remains one of the major open problems in planetary science

### Mars today: modern dynamics

- Thin 6 mbar $\mathrm{CO_2}$ atmosphere; dust is a key thermal and dynamical player
- **Global dust storms** (2007, 2018): feedback between dust heating, winds, and lofting; opacity can obscure the entire planet for months
- **Seasonal $\mathrm{CO_2}$ cycle**: $\sim$25% of the atmosphere by mass cycles through the polar caps each year
- **Recurring Slope Lineae (RSL)**: transient dark streaks on steep slopes; initial briny-water interpretation has been revised toward dry granular flow ({ref}`lecture06`)
- **Methane variability**: low background ($\sim$0.5 ppb) with transient spikes reported by Curiosity's TLS instrument, but ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter has reported much lower upper limits over the same periods. The disagreement is unresolved; current hypotheses invoke localised episodic releases from subsurface reservoirs, rapid destruction, or instrumental artefacts. Ambiguity is the honest state of this topic
- **Dust devils** and seasonal wind patterns observed by InSight pressure and seismic data

### Blackboard derivation (~10 min): Jeans escape flux from Mars

- **Setup**: start from the Maxwell--Boltzmann velocity distribution at the exobase (the altitude where the atmosphere becomes collisionless), with temperature $T_{\mathrm{exo}}$
- Define the **escape parameter**:

$$
\lambda = \frac{v_{\mathrm{esc}}^2}{v_{\mathrm{th}}^2} = \frac{GMm}{k_B T\, r_{\mathrm{exo}}}
$$

- Integrate the high-velocity tail above $v_{\mathrm{esc}}$ to obtain the **Jeans flux**:

$$
\Phi_J = n \left(\frac{k_B T}{2\pi m}\right)^{1/2} (1 + \lambda)\,e^{-\lambda}
$$

- **Application to Mars** ($T_{\mathrm{exo}} \sim 270$ K, $r_{\mathrm{exo}} \sim 3600$ km):
  - $\mathrm{H_2}$: $\lambda \approx 6$ $\rightarrow$ significant escape on geological timescales
  - $\mathrm{CO_2}$: $\lambda \approx 130$ $\rightarrow$ essentially zero thermal escape
- **Key insight**: Mars cannot retain light gases over Gyr timescales, but heavy gases survive Jeans escape. $\mathrm{CO_2}$ loss from Mars must be driven by **non-thermal processes** (solar wind pickup, photochemical, sputtering), not by the thermal tail of the distribution

### Mars atmospheric escape and loss

- **MAVEN** (NASA, 2014--): direct in-situ measurement of present-day escape rates
- Total loss: $\sim$1--2 kg/s of all species; dominant channel is photochemical escape of oxygen
- Enhanced during solar storms (factor of $\sim$10)
- **Integrated over 4 Gyr**: consistent with loss of $\sim$0.5--0.8 bar of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and $\sim$15--25 m global equivalent layer of water
- Directly ties the loss of the dynamo ({ref}`lecture04`) to the thinning of the atmosphere and desiccation of the surface

### Mars exploration history (part 1): early era through Spirit/Opportunity

- **Mariner 4** (1965): first close-up images, revealed cratered surface
- **Viking 1 \& 2** (1976): first successful landers, first biology experiments (results inconclusive)
- **Pathfinder + Sojourner** (1997): first rover on Mars
- **Mars Global Surveyor** (1997--2006): MOLA topography, crustal magnetism discovery
- **Mars Odyssey** (2001--), **Mars Express** (2003--), **MRO** (2006--): orbital science
- **Spirit \& Opportunity** (2004--2018): extensive rover traverses, evidence for past water at Gusev and Meridiani

### Mars exploration history (part 2): modern fleet and ongoing operations

- **Curiosity** + MSL (2012--): Gale crater, radiation environment, long-duration climate record
- **InSight** (2018--2022): seismology and interior structure (results feed into {ref}`lecture08`)
- **Perseverance + Ingenuity** (2021--): Jezero crater delta, sample caching; Ingenuity demonstrated powered flight on another planet
- **Tianwen-1 + Zhurong** (2021--): Chinese rover and orbiter, first mission to achieve orbit, lander, and rover in a single campaign
- **UAE Hope** (2021--): orbital atmospheric science
- **ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter** (2016--): methane sensitivity and aeronomy

### Mars habitability, past and present

- **Past**: Noachian Mars likely had surface or near-surface liquid water for intervals $>100$ Myr
- Conditions partially resembled early Earth: hydrothermal systems, mineral diversity, redox gradients, organic molecules preserved in mudstones
- **Present**: liquid water is thermodynamically unstable on the surface but may persist in the subsurface
- Radar reflections under the south polar cap (Orosei et al.\ 2018) suggested a subglacial liquid water lake, but the interpretation is contested; alternative explanations invoke conductive clays
- Subsurface habitability: microbes could in principle persist in deep aquifers, shielded from surface radiation and oxidants
- **Mars Sample Return**: NASA/ESA joint campaign, Perseverance caching tubes in Jezero
  - Original architecture targeted return in the early 2030s
  - **2024 re-baselining**: NASA announced a major cost and schedule overrun; architecture under review with industry and NASA study teams
  - Return date, lander design, and total cost all uncertain as of early 2026; the scientific imperative remains but the programmatic path is in flux
  - Will be the first planetary sample analysed for biosignatures with the full arsenal of terrestrial laboratory instruments


## Part 3: Comparative payoff

### Mercury vs Mars as limiting cases

- **Mercury**: too small, too close to the Sun, cooled rapidly, retained no atmosphere, core mostly solidified — yet it has an active dynamo and polar ice (against naive expectations)
- **Mars**: medium-sized, far from the Sun, cooled more slowly, once had water and a dynamo, then lost both — yet it has seasonal dynamics and ongoing geological activity today
- Both bracket Earth: smaller $\rightarrow$ faster cooling, lower gravity $\rightarrow$ more efficient escape, thinner atmosphere $\rightarrow$ less thermal inertia

### Size and distance set the trajectory

- **Planet size** controls the rate of interior cooling ($\tau \propto L^2/\kappa$, {ref}`lecture03`) and the strength of gravity (retention of atmospheres via escape parameter $\lambda$)
- **Distance from the star** sets the equilibrium temperature and the accessible volatile budget at formation
- These are the two fundamental parameters; many other properties are consequences

### The timing problem

- **Mercury**: dynamo died gradually but the core is still partly liquid today; the weak field persists. Why?
- **Mars**: dynamo died early (4.1--3.9 Ga; Acuña 1999, refined by Mittelholz et al.\ 2020); atmospheric loss followed rapidly
- **Earth**: dynamo still active thanks to inner core crystallisation ({ref}`lecture04`)
- **Venus**: no dynamo, but the shutdown time is unknown ({ref}`lecture09`)
- **Dynamo longevity correlates with atmospheric retention and surface habitability** across the rocky planets

### What makes a rocky planet habitable?

Requirements assembled from {ref}`lecture09` and this lecture:

1. **Liquid water** (needs temperature, pressure, and a sufficient volatile inventory)
2. **Active geology** to recycle volatiles (plate tectonics, heat-pipe volcanism, or cryovolcanism)
3. **Magnetic shielding** (contribution debated; important for atmospheric retention under active stellar wind)
4. **Long-term climate stability** (carbonate-silicate thermostat on Earth, or an equivalent feedback elsewhere)

- Earth ticks all four
- Venus and Mars failed on at least two each
- Mercury never had a chance (too small, too close, no volatiles)
- Forward reference to {ref}`lecture14` for the exoplanet habitability synthesis

### Recent advances and upcoming missions

- **Mars Sample Return** (NASA/ESA): Perseverance caching now; retrieval and return under architecture review after the 2024 re-baselining; return date uncertain but likely early-to-mid 2030s
- **BepiColombo** (ESA/JAXA): Mercury orbit insertion scheduled for 2026; first new Mercury mission since MESSENGER
- **ExoMars Rosalind Franklin** (ESA, $\sim$2028 after programmatic restart): subsurface drilling for biosignatures
- **JAXA MMX** (Martian Moons eXploration, launch 2026, return 2031): first sample return from the Mars system, targeting Phobos
- **Curiosity long-baseline results**: 12+ years of weather, radiation, and geochemistry data from Gale crater
- **Subsurface water radar**: MARSIS (Mars Express) and SHARAD (MRO) detections of buried reservoirs (interpretation contested)
- **Ingenuity helicopter legacy**: demonstrated powered flight in a non-Earth atmosphere; successor aerial platforms under study
- **Ongoing geochemistry from Perseverance**: igneous basement of Jezero, evidence for aqueous alteration, organic molecules


## Summary and takeaways

- **Mercury and Mars are nature's limiting cases** for rocky planets of varying size and distance from the Sun
- **Mercury's oddities** (metal-rich interior, persistent weak dynamo, polar ice) each point to a specific physical process (giant impact or mantle evaporation, thin convecting shell, obliquity-driven cold traps)
- **Mars' tragedy** is the textbook case of a world that had the ingredients for habitability, lost its dynamo, then lost its atmosphere and surface water — a cautionary tale for exoplanet habitability assessments
- **Size and distance** dominate the comparison; tectonic regime and dynamo longevity modulate the outcome
- **The next decade** will see MMX at Phobos, BepiColombo at Mercury, and (eventually) samples returning from Jezero — a golden era for comparative rocky-planet science
