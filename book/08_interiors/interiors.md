## Probing interiors

The surface of a planet reveals only the outermost skin of a body that extends thousands of kilometres inward. Understanding what lies beneath (the density structure, composition, phase state, and dynamics) is essential for explaining everything from magnetic fields ({ref}`Lecture 4 <lecture04>`) to volcanic activity ({ref}`Lecture 7 <lecture07>`) to long-term thermal evolution ({ref}`Lecture 3 <lecture03>`). Yet we cannot directly sample a planet's deep interior. Instead, we rely on three complementary observational tools {cite:p}`dePaterLissauer2010`.

**Seismology** is the most powerful probe of interior structure. When seismic waves generated by earthquakes (or marsquakes) travel through a planetary body, they refract and reflect at boundaries between layers of different density and composition. **P-waves** (primary, compressional) travel through both solids and liquids, while **S-waves** (secondary, shear) propagate only through solids. Because shear waves are absent beyond about 103° from an earthquake's epicentre, and because compressional waves leave a band of angular distances with no arrival at all ({numref}`fig:prem-shadow-zones`), Earth's outer core must be liquid {cite:p}`Dziewonski1981`. Such a band is called a **shadow zone**; {numref}`fig:seismic-shadow-zones` in {ref}`Lecture 4 <lecture04>` introduces the same argument schematically and names the individual ray phases. The travel times, amplitudes, and frequency content of seismic waves recorded at a network of stations can be inverted to determine the radial profiles of density $\rho(r)$, compressional wave speed $v_P(r)$, and shear wave speed $v_S(r)$ throughout the interior.

The geometry follows from a single conserved quantity. In a spherically symmetric body the **ray parameter**

$$
p = \frac{r \sin i}{v(r)}
$$

is constant along a ray, where $i$ is the angle between the ray and the local radial direction. A ray bottoms out at the radius where $r/v(r) = p$, and its angular distance is

$$
\Delta = 2 \int_{r_{\rm turn}}^{R} \frac{p \, \mathrm{d}r}{r \sqrt{\left(r/v\right)^2 - p^2}} .
$$

Integrating this through PREM, the reference model of Earth's radial structure presented in {ref}`the section on Earth's interior <earths-interior-section>`, reproduces the observed shadow zones without any free parameters.

**Gravity field measurements** constrain the distribution of mass within a planet. A perfectly spherical, homogeneous body produces a purely $1/r^2$ gravitational field. Real planets are oblate due to rotation, producing a measurable **gravitational quadrupole moment** $J_2$, the leading correction to the field beyond a simple $1/r^2$ dependence. The value of $J_2$ depends on how mass is distributed between the equator and the poles, which in turn depends on the density profile. Higher-order gravity harmonics reveal lateral density variations. Spacecraft in orbit (e.g., GRAIL at the Moon, Juno at Jupiter) measure gravity fields with extraordinary precision by tracking tiny Doppler shifts in their radio signals.

**The moment of inertia** is perhaps the single most diagnostic quantity for constraining a planet's internal mass distribution. For a body of mass $M$ and radius $R$, the dimensionless **moment of inertia factor** $C/MR^2$ (where $C$ is the polar moment of inertia) ranges from $2/5 = 0.4$ for a uniform-density sphere to lower values for bodies with mass concentrated toward the centre ({numref}`fig:moi-factor`). A measured value immediately tells us the degree of **differentiation**, the extent to which an initially more uniform body has separated into compositionally distinct layers, such as a dense metallic core and a lighter silicate mantle, through melting and gravitational settling:

| Body | $C/MR^2$ | Interpretation |
|------|:---------:|:--------------:|
| Uniform sphere | 0.400 | Reference value ($2/5$) |
| Moon | 0.393 | Weakly differentiated, small core |
| Mars | 0.364 | Moderately differentiated |
| Mercury | 0.346 | Large dense core |
| Earth | 0.331 | Strongly differentiated, iron core |

{numref}`fig:moi-two-layer` shows why differentiation lowers the factor: at fixed outer radius, moving mass into a dense central core removes it from large distances to the rotation axis, which suppresses the moment of inertia $C$ more strongly than the mass $M$.

The moment of inertia factor is determined observationally from two quantities: the gravitational oblateness $J_2$ (from spacecraft tracking) and the precession rate of the spin axis (or, equivalently, the forced **libration** amplitude, the small periodic oscillation of a body's orientation about its mean rotation, for bodies in **spin–orbit resonance**, i.e. rotating at a simple integer ratio to their orbital period). These are combined using the **Darwin–Radau relation**, which relates $C/MR^2$ to $J_2$ and the rotational flattening.

## Blackboard derivation: Moment of inertia factor

````{admonition} Blackboard derivation: $C/MR^2$ for uniform and differentiated bodies
:class: tip

**Goal:** Derive the moment of inertia factor for (a) a uniform sphere and (b) a two-layer differentiated body, and show how the measured value constrains internal structure.

**Step 1: Moment of inertia of a spherically symmetric body.**

The moment of inertia about the rotation axis is $C = \int r_\perp^2 \, dm$, where $r_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance from the axis. For a thin spherical shell of radius $r$ and density $\rho(r)$, the moment of inertia about a diameter is $dC = \frac{2}{3}r^2 \, dm$, where $dm = 4\pi r^2 \rho(r) \, dr$. Integrating over all shells:

$$
C = \frac{8\pi}{3} \int_0^R \rho(r) \, r^4 \, dr
$$ (eq:moi-integral)

<!-- Generated by scripts/figures/L08_interiors/fig_moi_shell_sketch.py -->
```{figure} figures/moi_shell_sketch.avif
:name: fig:moi-shell-sketch
:width: 420px
:align: center

The geometry behind {eq}`eq:moi-integral`. The body spins about the vertical axis at rate $\omega$. A mass element $dm$ at radius $r$ and colatitude $\theta$ sits at perpendicular distance $r_\perp = r\sin\theta$ from the axis and contributes $r_\perp^2\,dm$ to $C$. A thin shell of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ (shaded) carries the mass $4\pi r^2 \rho(r)\,dr$, and $\sin^2\theta$ averages to $2/3$ over the surface of a sphere, which gives $dC = \frac{2}{3}r^2\,dm$ and, integrated over all shells, {eq}`eq:moi-integral`. Course-original figure.
```

**Step 2: Uniform sphere** ($\rho = \text{const}$).

$$
C = \frac{8\pi}{3} \rho \int_0^R r^4 \, dr = \frac{8\pi}{3} \rho \frac{R^5}{5} = \frac{8\pi}{15} \rho R^5
$$

Using $M = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho$:

$$
\boxed{\frac{C}{MR^2} = \frac{8\pi\rho R^5 / 15}{(4\pi\rho R^3/3)\,R^2} = \frac{2}{5} = 0.400}
$$ (eq:moi-uniform)

**Step 3: Two-layer body** with a dense core (density $\rho_c$, radius $R_c$) and lighter mantle (density $\rho_m$, extending from $R_c$ to $R$):

$$
C = \frac{8\pi}{15} \left[ (\rho_c - \rho_m)\,R_c^5 + \rho_m\,R^5 \right]
$$

$$
M = \frac{4}{3}\pi \left[ (\rho_c - \rho_m)\,R_c^3 + \rho_m\,R^3 \right]
$$

Defining $x \equiv R_c/R$ and $f \equiv \rho_c/\rho_m$:

$$
\boxed{\frac{C}{MR^2} = \frac{2}{5} \cdot \frac{(f-1)\,x^5 + 1}{(f-1)\,x^3 + 1}}
$$ (eq:moi-two-layer)

When $f > 1$ (dense core), the factor multiplying $2/5$ is less than 1, because $x^5 < x^3$ for $0 < x < 1$. **Concentrating mass toward the centre pulls $C/MR^2$ below the uniform-sphere value of 0.4.** The effect is strongest at intermediate core sizes: as $x \to 1$ the body is again nearly uniform, and the factor climbs back toward 0.4.

**Worked example: Earth.** With $R_c/R \approx 3480/6371 \approx 0.546$ and $\rho_c/\rho_m \approx 11{,}000/4{,}400 \approx 2.5$:

$$
\frac{C}{MR^2} = \frac{2}{5} \times \frac{1.5 \times 0.049 + 1}{1.5 \times 0.163 + 1} = 0.4 \times \frac{1.073}{1.244} \approx 0.345
$$

The measured value is $C/MR^2 = 0.3307$, close to our two-layer estimate, with the remaining difference due to the continuous density increase within each layer (PREM gives an additional $\sim 5\%$ density jump across the inner-core boundary).

**Key insight:** A single number, $C/MR^2$, immediately distinguishes an undifferentiated rubble pile (0.4) from a strongly differentiated body like Earth (0.33). This is often the first constraint available for a newly characterised planet or moon.
````


## Equations of state

To construct a quantitative model of a planetary interior, we need an **equation of state** (EOS) that relates pressure $P$, density $\rho$, and temperature $T$ at every depth. The starting point is **hydrostatic equilibrium**: the condition that pressure at each depth supports the weight of the overlying material. In spherical symmetry {cite:p}`Turcotte2002`:

$$
\frac{dP}{dr} = -\rho(r)\,g(r) = -\frac{G\,m(r)\,\rho(r)}{r^2}
$$ (eq:hydrostatic-interior)

where $m(r) = 4\pi\int_0^r \rho(r')\,r'^2\,dr'$ is the mass enclosed within radius $r$ and $g(r) = Gm(r)/r^2$ is the local gravitational acceleration. This equation, combined with an EOS, can be integrated inward from the surface to determine the pressure, density, and composition at every depth.

For a rough estimate of the **central pressure** of a planet, we can assume uniform density $\bar{\rho} = 3M/(4\pi R^3)$:

$$
P_c \approx \frac{3\,G\,M^2}{8\pi\,R^4}
$$ (eq:central-pressure)

For Earth ($M = 5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg, $R = 6371$ km), this gives $P_c \approx 170$ GPa, roughly half the actual value of $\sim$360 GPa, because the real density increases substantially toward the centre {cite:p}`Dziewonski1981`. {numref}`fig:central-pressure` extends this comparison across the solar system: every body plots above the line of equality, and the gap grows from a few per cent for the nearly homogeneous Moon to a factor of about three for Jupiter, whose deep interior is both strongly compressed and centrally condensed.

What form should the EOS itself take? The simplest equation of state in physics is the **ideal gas law**, $P = \rho\,k_B T/(\mu m_u)$, where $\mu$ is the mean molecular mass in atomic mass units. It describes planetary atmospheres well, and even most of the Sun's interior, because in a dilute gas (and in a fully ionised plasma) pressure is *thermal*: it is the momentum flux of freely moving particles, so density adjusts in direct proportion to $P$ and in inverse proportion to $T$. Planetary interiors are condensed matter, and there the ideal gas law fails completely. In rock, iron, or ice the interatomic spacing is set by the balance between electrostatic attraction and the quantum-mechanical repulsion of overlapping electron shells, an energy balance of several electronvolts per atom, whereas the thermal energy at mantle temperatures is only $k_B T \approx 0.2$ eV at 2000 K. Pressure in a solid or liquid planet is therefore *elastic* rather than thermal: it takes tens of gigapascals to compress rock by a few per cent, and the density depends only weakly on temperature. The numbers make the failure vivid. At lower-mantle conditions ($P \approx 100$ GPa, $T \approx 2500$ K, $\mu \approx 20$ for MgSiO$_3$), the ideal gas law would predict $\rho \approx 10^5$ kg m$^{-3}$, roughly twenty times the true $\sim$5000 kg m$^{-3}$; at Earth's surface ($P = 1$ bar, $T = 300$ K) it would predict $\rho \approx 1$ kg m$^{-3}$, three thousand times too *low* for the same rock. No single correction bridges these failures: planetary interiors need equations of state built for condensed matter, anchored in laboratory compression experiments and quantum-mechanical calculations.

{numref}`fig:eos-paleos` shows what such condensed-matter equations of state look like for the three canonical planet-building materials: iron, silicate (MgSiO$_3$), and water, drawn from the PALEOS **multiphase** EOS framework {cite:p}`Attia2026`, which combines laboratory compression data and quantum-mechanical calculations into thermodynamically consistent tables spanning planetary interior conditions. Three lessons stand out. First, condensed matter is stiff: along the 300 K isotherm each material must be squeezed to tens of gigapascals before its density rises appreciably, and even at $10^4$ GPa, deep-giant-planet pressure, iron is compressed only about five-fold. Second, the density does not rise smoothly: discrete steps mark pressure-induced **phase transitions** to more densely packed crystal structures ($\alpha$- to $\varepsilon$-iron; enstatite through bridgmanite to post-perovskite in the silicate; the high-pressure ices VII and X in water), the same transitions we will meet as seismic discontinuities inside Earth ([Phase transitions](phase-transitions-section)). Third, temperature matters only where pressure is low: heating iron from 300 to 4000 K lowers its density by 12 per cent at 0.1 GPa but by only 2 per cent at Earth's central pressure, where compression dominates. Water is the instructive exception: at 4000 K and low pressure it is a supercritical fluid twenty times less dense than the cold liquid, thermal-pressure behaviour close to the ideal-gas limit; its hot and cold branches still differ by more than 10 per cent at 100 GPa and converge to the few-per-cent level only near Earth's central pressure. Which regime a material occupies therefore depends on where in the planet it sits, and equations of state like these are the foundation on which mass-radius relations for exoplanets are built ({ref}`Lecture 13 <lecture13>`).

At the pressures and temperatures found in planetary interiors ($P \sim 1$–$10^4$ GPa, $T \sim 10^3$–$10^4$ K), material behaviour differs drastically from surface conditions. The **bulk modulus** $K = -V\,dP/dV = \rho\,dP/d\rho$ describes a material's resistance to compression. {cite:t}`Birch1952` established an empirical relationship between compressional wave velocity $v_P$ and density for silicates and oxides ({numref}`fig:birchs-law`), known as **Birch's law**, which remains a cornerstone of mineral physics.

The **seismic parameter** $\phi \equiv K_S/\rho = v_P^2 - \frac{4}{3}v_S^2$ (where $K_S$ is the adiabatic bulk modulus) connects seismological observables directly to the EOS. The **Adams–Williamson equation** uses this to relate the density gradient to the seismic velocities:

$$
\frac{d\rho}{dr} = -\frac{\rho(r)\,g(r)}{\phi(r)}
$$

This equation allows seismologists to determine the density profile from measured wave speeds, provided the composition is approximately uniform within each layer and temperature effects are small. Tested against the Preliminary Reference Earth Model (PREM), the standard radial profile of Earth's density and seismic wave speeds assembled from global earthquake data {cite:p}`Dziewonski1981` and presented in detail in {ref}`the next section <earths-interior-section>`, the Adams-Williamson relation reproduces the smooth lower-mantle density rise from self-compression alone but breaks down at the core-mantle boundary, where a discontinuous density jump signals the compositional change from silicate mantle to iron-alloy core ({numref}`fig:adams-williamson`).

**The crust** is Earth's outermost solid layer, varying from $\sim$5–10 km thick beneath the oceans (dense basaltic rock, $\rho \approx 3000$ kg m$^{-3}$) to $\sim$30–70 km thick beneath the continents (lighter granitic rock, $\rho \approx 2700$ kg m$^{-3}$). Despite being the layer we inhabit and study most directly, the crust constitutes less than 1% of Earth's total mass.

**The mantle** extends from the base of the crust (the **Mohorovičić discontinuity**, or Moho) down to 2891 km depth. It is composed primarily of silicate minerals rich in magnesium and iron. Key seismic discontinuities divide it into:

- **Upper mantle** (to ~410 km): dominated by olivine ($\mathrm{(Mg,Fe)_2SiO_4}$), with densities of 3300–3700 kg m$^{-3}$
- **Transition zone** (410–660 km): olivine transforms to higher-pressure polymorphs, denser crystal structures of the same chemical composition (wadsleyite, then ringwoodite; see [Phase transitions](phase-transitions-section) below)
- **Lower mantle** (660–2891 km): dominated by bridgmanite ($\mathrm{(Mg,Fe)SiO_3}$) and ferropericlase ($\mathrm{(Mg,Fe)O}$), with densities reaching $\sim$5570 kg m$^{-3}$ at the base

**The outer core** (2891–5150 km depth) is a liquid alloy of iron and nickel with $\sim$10% lighter elements (likely sulphur, oxygen, silicon, or hydrogen). Its liquid state is proven by the absence of S-waves and by the existence of Earth's magnetic dynamo ({ref}`Lecture 4 <lecture04>`), which requires a convecting, electrically conducting fluid. Temperatures range from $\sim$4000 K at the top to $\sim$5000 K at the base.

**The inner core** (5150–6371 km depth) is solid iron–nickel, crystallised from the cooling outer core. Despite temperatures of $\sim$5000–6000 K, the enormous pressure ($\sim$330–360 GPa) keeps it solid. The inner core grows at an estimated rate of $\sim$0.5 mm yr$^{-1}$ as Earth slowly cools, and the latent heat released by this crystallisation is an important energy source for driving the geodynamo {cite:p}`Schubert2001`.


## Mantle rheology

Although Earth's mantle is **solid** (it transmits S-waves), it flows over geological timescales. This seemingly paradoxical behaviour arises because the mantle's **viscosity** (a measure of resistance to flow) is finite, around $10^{21}$ Pa·s for the upper mantle (compare $\sim$10$^{-3}$ Pa·s for water and $\sim$10$^{12}$ Pa·s for glacial ice). At temperatures above roughly half the melting point, solid-state **creep** mechanisms allow crystals to deform slowly by the diffusion of atoms along grain boundaries or through the crystal lattice {cite:p}`Karato2008`.

Whether the mantle convects vigorously is governed by the **Rayleigh number** (introduced in {ref}`Lecture 3 <lecture03>`):

$$
\text{Ra} = \frac{\alpha\,\rho\,g\,\Delta T\,d^3}{\kappa\,\eta}
$$

where $\alpha$ is the thermal expansivity, $\Delta T$ the temperature difference driving convection, $d$ the layer thickness, $\kappa$ the thermal diffusivity, and $\eta$ the dynamic viscosity. For Earth's mantle, $\text{Ra} \sim 10^7$–$10^8$, far above the critical value $\text{Ra}_c \approx 10^3$, so the mantle convects vigorously, driving plate tectonics at the surface ({ref}`Lecture 7 <lecture07>`).

The pattern of mantle convection was long framed as a dichotomy ({numref}`fig:convection-regimes`). **Whole-mantle convection** models have material circulating from the surface to the core–mantle boundary, while **layered convection** models propose that the 660 km discontinuity impedes flow, creating separate upper- and lower-mantle circulation cells. Seismic tomography (imaging the mantle's 3D velocity structure) has since resolved the central question: cold subducted slabs can be traced from subduction zones deep into the lower mantle, with some slab segments apparently sinking to the base of the mantle {cite:p}`VanDerHilst1997`, and broad low-velocity conduits rise from the core–mantle boundary beneath major hotspots {cite:p}`French2015`. Mass evidently crosses the 660 km boundary in both directions, so strictly layered convection is no longer tenable.

The boundary still shapes the flow, however. Global tomographic surveys show that slab behaviour at 660 km varies regionally: some slabs penetrate directly into the lower mantle, others flatten and stagnate above the discontinuity, and others are trapped between 660 and 1000 km depth before resuming their descent {cite:p}`Fukao2013`. The debate has therefore shifted from "one layer or two?" to the nature and longevity of chemical heterogeneity within a whole-mantle circulation: the two continent-sized **large low shear velocity provinces** (LLSVPs) at the base of the mantle beneath Africa and the Pacific may be thermochemically distinct piles {cite:p}`McNamara2019`, intrinsically viscous, silica-enriched domains may resist mixing and persist at depths of roughly 1000 to 2200 km {cite:p}`Ballmer2017`, and the transition zone may act as a water filter, with dehydration melting of upwelling material at the 410 km discontinuity separating trace elements while still permitting whole-mantle flow {cite:p}`Bercovici2003`.

## Phase transitions

As pressure and temperature increase with depth, minerals undergo **phase transitions**, i.e. rearrangements of their crystal structure to denser configurations. These transitions produce the seismic discontinuities that define the boundaries within Earth's mantle {cite:p}`Turcotte2002`:

| Depth (km) | Transition | Density change |
|:----------:|:---------:|:--------------:|
| 410 | Olivine → wadsleyite | $\sim$5% increase |
| 520 | Wadsleyite → ringwoodite | $\sim$2% increase |
| 660 | Ringwoodite → bridgmanite + ferropericlase | $\sim$8% increase |
| $\sim$2700 | Bridgmanite → post-perovskite | $\sim$1–2% increase |

The **410 km discontinuity** marks the transition of olivine ($\mathrm{(Mg,Fe)_2SiO_4}$) to wadsleyite, a denser crystal structure with the same chemical composition. At 660 km, the most dramatic transformation occurs: ringwoodite (the highest-pressure olivine polymorph) breaks down into bridgmanite ($\mathrm{(Mg,Fe)SiO_3}$, a perovskite-structured phase, a dense mineral structure common at high pressure) plus ferropericlase ($\mathrm{(Mg,Fe)O}$). This **660 km discontinuity** is the most important internal boundary within the mantle, marking the transition from upper to lower mantle. Its negative Clapeyron slope (the boundary pressure decreases with increasing temperature) means it partially resists the passage of convecting material, influencing the style of mantle convection.

Near the base of the mantle ($\sim$2700 km depth), bridgmanite transforms to **post-perovskite**, a phase discovered in laboratory experiments in 2004 {cite:p}`Murakami2004`. This transition may explain the seismically anomalous **D" layer** just above the core–mantle boundary, where seismic velocities show complex lateral variations. {numref}`fig:mantle-phase-diagram` summarises the principal phase boundaries against an approximate Earth geotherm.

These phase transitions are not unique to Earth. Any sufficiently large rocky body will experience them at appropriate pressures, though the specific depths depend on the body's size, gravity, and thermal state. Mars, for example, likely has an olivine-to-wadsleyite transition in its deep mantle, while the Moon is too small to reach the required pressures.

**The Moon** ($C/MR^2 = 0.393$, $\bar{\rho} = 3346$ kg m$^{-3}$) is only weakly differentiated. Apollo seismometers (1969–1977), reanalysed with modern processing techniques, revealed a crust $\sim$34–43 km thick, a silicate mantle extending to $\sim$1400 km depth, and a small partially molten core with radius $\sim$200–380 km (only $\sim$10–20% of the Moon's radius: the lower bound from GRAIL and lunar laser ranging gravity inversions {cite:p}`Williams2014`, the upper bound from Apollo seismic reanalysis {cite:p}`Weber2011`; {numref}`fig:moon-interior`). The near-undifferentiated state ($C/MR^2$ close to 0.4) and low mean density are consistent with a body that formed primarily from silicate mantle material, supporting the giant impact hypothesis for the Moon's origin ({ref}`Lecture 2 <lecture02>`).

**Mars** ($C/MR^2 = 0.364$, $\bar{\rho} = 3934$ kg m$^{-3}$) was transformed from a one-data-point problem into a seismically characterised planet by NASA's **InSight mission** {cite:p}`Banerdt2020` (2018–2022; {numref}`fig:mars-interior`). The SEIS seismometer detected hundreds of marsquakes, revealing a layered internal structure {cite:p}`Khan2021,Stahler2021`:

- A **crust** 24–72 km thick (varying between the thin northern lowlands and thick southern highlands) {cite:p}`Knapmeyer-Endrun2021`
- A **mantle** extending to $\sim$1560 km depth (i.e. to the top of the core)
- A **liquid core** of radius $\sim$1830 km ($\sim$54% of Mars's radius; later revised downward, see below), composed of iron alloyed with sulphur and other light elements

Mars's core occupies a comparable fraction of the planet's radius (54%) to Earth's outer core (55%), but is far less dense ($\sim$6000 kg m$^{-3}$ vs $\sim$10,900 kg m$^{-3}$), indicating a higher fraction of light elements, particularly sulphur ({numref}`fig:mars-wedge`). The seismic velocity profiles ({numref}`fig:mars-vp-vs`) directly capture this contrast: $v_S$ drops to zero across the core-mantle boundary, while $v_P$ steps down because the liquid Fe-S alloy has a smaller bulk modulus than the overlying silicate mantle. Two 2023 reanalyses using marsquakes recorded on the planet's far side reinterpret the Stähler 2021 boundary as the top of a molten silicate layer ({cite:t}`Khan2023`: $150 \pm 15$ km thick) rather than the metallic core itself. This revises the iron core radius downward to $\sim$1650–1675 km and its density upward to $\sim$6470–6650 kg m$^{-3}$ {cite:p}`Khan2023,Samuel2023`. The absence of a present-day global magnetic field suggests the core is no longer convecting vigorously enough to sustain a dynamo, although remnant crustal magnetisation records an ancient field ({ref}`Lecture 4 <lecture04>`).

**Mercury** ($C/MR^2 = 0.346$, $\bar{\rho} = 5427$ kg m$^{-3}$) presents a puzzle: it has the highest **uncompressed density** of any terrestrial planet (the density its material would have at zero pressure, correcting for self-compression), implying a very large iron core ({numref}`fig:mercury-interior`). ## Giant planet interiors

Jupiter and Saturn are fundamentally different from rocky planets: composed primarily of hydrogen and helium, they lack distinct solid surfaces. Their "surfaces" are conventionally defined as the 1-bar pressure level, and below this the atmosphere transitions continuously into an increasingly dense interior ({numref}`fig:jupiter-interior`) {cite:p}`FortneyNettelmann2010`.

```{figure} figures/jupiter_interior.avif

Below, a helium rain layer forms a stably stratified region where helium droplets settle inward. **Jupiter** ($M = 318\,\Mearth$, $R = 11.2\,\Rearth$) has been characterised with unprecedented precision by the **Juno mission** (2016–present), which measures Jupiter's gravity field to high accuracy. The interior can be described in broad terms:

- **Molecular hydrogen envelope** (outer ~15% by radius): $\mathrm{H_2}$ gas transitioning to liquid, $\rho \sim 0.2$–$1$ g cm$^{-3}$
- **Metallic hydrogen layer** (extending to ~80% of the radius): at pressures above $\sim$1–4 Mbar ($10^{11}$–$4 \times 10^{11}$ Pa), hydrogen undergoes a gradual transition to a **metallic state** where electrons are delocalised and the material conducts electricity. This conducting metallic hydrogen is the source of Jupiter's powerful magnetic field ({ref}`Lecture 4 <lecture04>`)
- **Core region**: Juno's gravity data show that most of the heavy elements (rock and ice) are not concentrated in a small compact core but are instead spread over a large **dilute core** extending to perhaps 30–50% of Jupiter's radius ({numref}`fig:jupiter-z`) {cite:p}`Wahl2017`. A small pure heavy-element core of between 1 and 5 $\Mearth$ at the very centre is still allowed by the gravity data, but is not required by them {cite:p}`Helled2022`. The dilute core may be a relic of the formation process or the result of erosion of an initially compact core by convective mixing

Central conditions reach approximately 4000 GPa and 20,000 K. Jupiter radiates roughly 1.7 times the energy it receives from the Sun, indicating substantial internal heat production from ongoing gravitational contraction (the **Kelvin–Helmholtz mechanism**).

**Saturn** ($M = 95\,\Mearth$, $R = 9.4\,\Rearth$) shares Jupiter's overall structure but with some key differences ({numref}`fig:saturn-interior`, {numref}`fig:saturn-schematic`). Saturn's lower mass means pressures are lower throughout, so the metallic hydrogen transition occurs deeper. Saturn also has a larger proportional heavy-element enrichment. A notable process in Saturn's interior is **helium rain**: as the planet cools, helium becomes immiscible in metallic hydrogen at $P \approx 2$ Mbar and forms droplets that settle toward the centre, releasing gravitational energy and explaining Saturn's unexpectedly high heat output {cite:p}`MankovichFortney2020`. Saturn radiates about 1.8 times the absorbed solar flux, more than expected from simple contraction alone. Recent ring seismology with Cassini data revealed that Saturn, like Jupiter, harbours a **diffuse heavy-element core**: a stably stratified region extending to $\sim$60% of the planet's radius containing $\sim$17 $\Mearth$ of heavy elements {cite:p}`Mankovich2021`.

## Ice giant interiors

Uranus ($M = 14.5\,\Mearth$) and Neptune ($M = 17.1\,\Mearth$) are often called **ice giants** to distinguish them from the gas giants, because a large fraction of their mass consists of heavier molecules (primarily water ($\mathrm{H_2O}$), ammonia ($\mathrm{NH_3}$), and methane ($\mathrm{CH_4}$)) rather than hydrogen and helium {cite:p}`FortneyNettelmann2010`.

The canonical three-layer model consists of:

1. **Hydrogen–helium envelope** (outermost $\sim$20% by mass): molecular gas transitioning to denser fluid
2. **Ice/fluid layer** ($\sim$60–70% by mass): a hot, dense fluid of water, ammonia, and methane at pressures of $\sim$10–500 GPa and temperatures of $\sim$2000–7000 K. Despite the name "ice," this material is a hot ionic fluid; in part of the layer it may transition into a predicted **superionic** state, in which oxygen atoms form a crystalline lattice while hydrogen ions diffuse freely and the material conducts electricity, although the existence and depth range of this phase inside Uranus and Neptune remain uncertain
3. **Rocky core** ($\sim$10–20% by mass): silicates and iron at the centre, though the boundary with the ice layer may be gradational ({numref}`fig:icegiant-interior`)

The magnetic fields of Uranus and Neptune are strikingly unusual: both are strongly **tilted** relative to the rotation axis (59° for Uranus, 47° for Neptune) and **offset** from the planet's centre. This contrasts sharply with the roughly dipolar, axially aligned fields of Earth, Jupiter, and Saturn. The explanation likely involves the geometry of the conducting region: rather than being generated in a deep metallic hydrogen shell (as in Jupiter), the ice giant dynamos may operate in the shallow, thin conducting ice layer, producing more complex field geometries.

Neptune emits $\sim$2.6 times the solar energy it absorbs, while Uranus has essentially **no measurable internal heat excess**, a long-standing puzzle. Proposed explanations include compositional gradients that inhibit convection in Uranus's interior, trapping heat within, while Neptune convects more freely. This remains one of the key open questions motivating future ice giant missions, and we examine it further in {ref}`Lecture 11 <lecture11>`.


## Icy moon interiors

Among the most remarkable discoveries in planetary science is that several moons in the outer solar system harbour **subsurface liquid water oceans** beneath their icy shells ({numref}`fig:europa-interior`, {numref}`fig:enceladus-interior`, {numref}`fig:titan-interior`). These ocean worlds are prime targets for astrobiology because they provide the three key ingredients for life: liquid water, chemical energy, and organic molecules ({ref}`Lecture 7 <lecture07>`; {ref}`Lecture 14 <lecture14>` develops the astrobiological case) {cite:p}`NimmoPappalardo2016`.

**Europa** ($R = 1561$ km, $\rho = 3013$ kg m$^{-3}$) has an ice shell estimated at a few to a few tens of kilometres thick, overlying a global ocean roughly 100 km deep {cite:p}`NimmoPappalardo2016`. The evidence comes from multiple sources: magnetic induction measurements by the Galileo spacecraft (a time-varying magnetic field induced by Jupiter's rotating magnetosphere, requiring a near-surface conducting layer), surface geology (disrupted ice blocks in **chaos terrain** that resemble terrestrial sea ice), and gravity data. **Tidal heating** (frictional heating generated as a moon's interior flexes under a planet's changing gravitational pull along an eccentric orbit) from Europa's orbital eccentricity, maintained by the Laplace resonance with Io and Ganymede ({numref}`fig:tidal-resonance`), provides the energy to keep the ocean liquid. The upcoming **Europa Clipper** mission (launched 2024) will characterise Europa's ice shell and ocean in unprecedented detail {cite:p}`HowellPappalardo2020`.

The existence of these ocean worlds demonstrates that the classical **habitable zone** (defined by the distance from a star where liquid water can exist on a surface) is far too narrow a concept. Tidal heating, radiogenic heating ({ref}`Lecture 3 <lecture03>`), and insulating ice shells can maintain liquid water far beyond the snow line, vastly expanding the potential environments for life in the universe.


## Recent advances

NASA's InSight mission (2018–2022) delivered landmark results for Mars interior science. Seismology from the SEIS instrument detected marsquakes and measured seismic wave travel times through the planet, revealing a liquid iron-alloy core with radius $\sim$1830 km {cite:p}`Stahler2021` (later revised downward to $\sim$1650–1675 km, see above), an upper-mantle structure to $\sim$800 km depth with a low-velocity zone consistent with a thick thermal **lithosphere** (the planet's rigid outer shell, comprising the crust and uppermost mantle) of $\sim$400–600 km {cite:p}`Khan2021`, and a crust 24–72 km thick {cite:p}`Knapmeyer-Endrun2021`. Combining these results constrains the Mars mantle to be relatively thin compared to Earth's. These measurements provided the first seismological constraints on another planet's interior since Apollo, confirming that Mars is differentiated and has a proportionally similar but lower-density core compared to Earth ({ref}`Lecture 10 <lecture10>`).

For giant planets, Juno's precise gravity measurements have strengthened the case for a **dilute core** in Jupiter: rather than a compact rocky/icy core, Jupiter's heavy elements appear to be distributed over the inner $\sim$30–50% of the planet's radius, gradually mixing into the metallic hydrogen envelope {cite:p}`Wahl2017`. This result challenges the traditional core accretion picture in which the core remains distinct, and has motivated new models of core erosion and primordial mixing during giant planet formation ({ref}`Lecture 2 <lecture02>`).

NASA's Europa Clipper mission, launched in 2024, will make dozens of close flybys of Europa to characterise its ice shell thickness, ocean salinity, and surface geology {cite:p}`HowellPappalardo2020`. Ice-penetrating radar, gravity measurements, and magnetometry will provide the most detailed constraints yet on the internal structure of an ocean world, directly testing models of subsurface ocean circulation and habitability ({ref}`Lecture 14 <lecture14>`).
