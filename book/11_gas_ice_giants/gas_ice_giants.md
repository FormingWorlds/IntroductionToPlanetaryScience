## Part 1: The gas giants — Jupiter & Saturn

### Gas giant overview: what makes them different

- Jupiter: 318 $\Mearth$, 11.2 $\Rearth$; Saturn: 95 $\Mearth$, 9.4 $\Rearth$
- Composition dominated by $\mathrm{H_2}$ and He ($\sim$90% by mass); heavy-element enrichment varies
- **No solid surface**; pressure and temperature rise smoothly with depth
- Rapid rotation: Jupiter $\sim$9.9 hr, Saturn $\sim$10.7 hr $\rightarrow$ strong Coriolis effects, banded atmospheres
- Both emit more energy than they receive from the Sun (internal heat from contraction; additionally helium rain in Saturn)

### Jupiter interior structure

- Molecular $\mathrm{H_2}$/He envelope from the cloud tops down to $\sim$0.85 $R_J$
- Transition to **metallic hydrogen** at $\sim$100 GPa
- **Dilute core** (recap from {ref}`lecture02` and {ref}`lecture08`): heavy elements distributed over inner 30--50% of radius, total heavy-element content $\sim$20--40 $\Mearth$
- Central conditions: $\sim$4000 GPa, $\sim$20{,}000 K
- Juno gravity constraints now refined to $J_{12}$ (Militzer et al.\ 2022)
- Open questions: formation pathway compatible with a dilute core; rotation profile at depth

### Jupiter atmosphere and weather

- Cloud layers: $\mathrm{NH_3}$ (top, $\sim$0.5--1 bar), $\mathrm{NH_4SH}$ ($\sim$2--3 bar), $\mathrm{H_2O}$ ($\sim$5--7 bar) — recap from {ref}`lecture06`
- Zonal bands: zones (rising, bright, ammonia clouds) and belts (sinking, darker)
- $\sim$15 alternating jets, wind speeds up to $\sim$180 m/s
- **Great Red Spot**: anticyclonic storm persisting for $\gtrsim$350 years; shrinking over the past century
- **Great Blue Spot**: magnetic flux anomaly (recap from {ref}`lecture04`)
- Aurorae: powered by the magnetosphere plus Io volcanic mass loading ({ref}`lecture04`)

### Io

- Most volcanically active body in the solar system
- $\sim$400 active volcanic centres; $\sim$10$^{14}$ W of tidal heat dissipation (recap from {ref}`lecture03`)
- Surface resurfaced on $\sim$Myr timescales; essentially no impact craters
- Connects to the Laplace resonance and tidal heating from {ref}`lecture03`
- Juno extended mission recent results: global magma ocean inferred from tidal Love number (Park et al.\ 2024), polar heat anomalies

### Europa

- Icy shell $\sim$15--25 km over a global $\sim$100 km deep liquid water ocean
- Laplace resonance maintains orbital eccentricity, sustains tidal heating
- Chaos terrain, lineae, young surface age ($\sim$40--90 Myr)
- Possible water vapour plumes (Hubble detections, contested)
- **Europa Clipper** launched October 2024, arrives 2030 for $\sim$50 close flybys

### Ganymede

- Largest moon in the solar system ($R = 2634$ km, larger than Mercury)
- Only moon with an intrinsic dynamo (recap from {ref}`lecture04` and {ref}`lecture08`)
- Differentiated: iron core, silicate mantle, ice shell with subsurface ocean ($\sim$100 km deep ocean beneath $\sim$150 km ice shell)
- Hubble UV aurora observations confirm the ocean (Saur et al.\ 2015)
- **JUICE** mission will enter Ganymede orbit in 2034 — the first orbiter of a moon other than Earth's

### Callisto

- Outermost Galilean moon; least differentiated ($C/MR^2 \approx 0.36$, between the Moon and Europa)
- Heavily cratered ancient surface; looks superficially like Mercury or the Moon
- Possible subsurface ocean (induced magnetic field signature from Galileo spacecraft)
- **Outside the Laplace resonance** $\rightarrow$ no sustained tidal heating
- Lowest radiation environment of the four Galileans (outside Jupiter's main radiation belts)
- "Boring but habitable": often considered a future crewed-mission target

### Jupiter's rings and small moons

- Faint dusty ring system discovered by Voyager 1 (1979)
- Source: micrometeorite impacts on Amalthea, Adrastea, Metis, Thebe (gardening of small inner moons)
- Unlike Saturn's rings, Jupiter's are mostly dust, not ice $\rightarrow$ dark and faint

### Saturn interior and rotation

- Composition similar to Jupiter but lower mass $\rightarrow$ lower central pressure, different interior structure
- **Helium rain** at $\sim$1--3 Mbar: He becomes immiscible with H and settles inward, releasing gravitational potential energy $\rightarrow$ explains Saturn's excess luminosity ($\sim$2$\times$ absorbed flux)
- Dilute core probable, mass $\sim$10--20 $\Mearth$
- **Rotation period uncertain** because no solid surface and no strong magnetic asymmetry; current best estimate $\sim$10.7 hr (from Cassini ring seismology)
- **Dipole field almost perfectly aligned** with the rotation axis — an anomaly that challenges standard dynamo theory (asymmetry is generally required for dynamo generation)

### Saturn atmosphere and weather

- Similar cloud-layer structure to Jupiter ($\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{NH_4SH}$, $\mathrm{H_2O}$) but deeper due to lower gravity $\rightarrow$ less visible contrast
- Zonal jets even faster than Jupiter's: equatorial jet reaches $\sim$400 m/s
- **Hexagonal polar jet**: a standing Rossby wave at $\sim$78°N, discovered by Voyager, mapped in detail by Cassini (recap from {ref}`lecture06`)
- **Great White Storms**: periodic massive convective outbursts ($\sim$30-year interval); last major event in 2010
- Seasonal storms driven by Saturn's 26.7° axial tilt

### Saturn's rings — structure and composition

- Seven main rings (A through G) and many gaps (Cassini Division, Encke Gap, and others)
- Radial extent from 67{,}000 km (inner D ring) to 140{,}000 km (outer F ring) — mostly within the Roche limit
- Composition: $>$95% water ice; particle sizes from cm to tens of metres
- Total mass $\sim$$1.5 \times 10^{19}$ kg (comparable to Saturn's moon Mimas)
- **Ring-moon interactions**: shepherd moons (Pandora, Prometheus), density waves, gap clearing
- Cassini Division is maintained by 2:1 mean-motion resonance with Mimas

### Saturn's rings — age and evolution

- **Young-rings consensus** (Iess et al.\ 2019; Crida et al.\ 2019): based on Cassini grand-finale gravity measurements, rings are $\sim$100 Myr old, much younger than Saturn itself
- Likely source: tidal disruption of a moderate-sized moon, or a late captured body
- **"Ring rain"**: rings are actively losing mass into Saturn's atmosphere at rates implying lifetimes of $\sim$100 Myr (Waite et al.\ 2018; O'Donoghue et al.\ 2019)
- Rings are therefore a transient feature — we are lucky to live in the narrow window when they exist
- Debate ongoing: some studies argue for older primordial rings with ongoing pollution (Wisdom et al.\ 2022 on dynamics); uncertainty is a real part of current research
- Pedagogical lesson: solar system bodies can change dramatically on sub-Gyr timescales, not just at formation

### Blackboard derivation (~10 min): The Roche limit

- **Setup**: a fluid satellite of density $\rho_s$ orbiting a planet of density $\rho_p$ and radius $R_p$ at distance $d$
- Balance the tidal force from the planet across the satellite against the satellite's self-gravity
- **Tidal acceleration** across the satellite: $\Delta a_{\mathrm{tidal}} \approx \dfrac{2 G M_p R_s}{d^3}$
- **Satellite self-gravity** at its surface: $a_{\mathrm{self}} = \dfrac{G M_s}{R_s^2} = \dfrac{4}{3}\pi G \rho_s R_s$
- Equate the two, use $M_p = (4/3)\pi \rho_p R_p^3$, and solve for $d$:

$$
d_R \approx 2.46\, R_p \left(\frac{\rho_p}{\rho_s}\right)^{1/3}
$$

- **Apply to Saturn's rings** ($R_p = 58{,}232$ km, $\rho_p = 687$ kg/m$^3$, $\rho_s \approx 1000$ kg/m$^3$ for ice): $d_R \approx 126{,}000$ km, matching the outer edge of the A ring well
- **Key insight**: rings exist interior to $d_R$ because the particles cannot coalesce gravitationally under tidal stress; they orbit as a flattened disk maintained by mutual collisions

### Titan

- Second largest moon in the solar system after Ganymede; the only moon with a substantial atmosphere
- Thick $\mathrm{N_2}$ atmosphere ($\sim$1.5 bar surface pressure, $\sim$50% denser than Earth's)
- **Methane hydrological cycle**: lakes and seas near the poles, methane rain, dendritic river valleys (recap from {ref}`lecture06`)
- Subsurface water--ammonia ocean beneath an ice shell (Cassini gravity and tidal deformation measurements)
- Surface temperature 94 K — near the methane triple point
- Organic haze from $\mathrm{CH_4}$ photolysis $\rightarrow$ tholins, aerosols, and dune material
- **Dragonfly** rotorcraft mission: launch 2028, arrival 2034, will explore the Selk crater region

### Enceladus

- Tiny moon ($R = 252$ km) with disproportionate scientific significance
- Tiger-stripe fractures near the south pole
- **Active geysers** discovered by Cassini (2005): water vapour, ice particles, salts, silica nanoparticles, organic molecules, molecular $\mathrm{H_2}$ (Postberg 2018, 2023)
- Global subsurface ocean beneath a 20--30 km ice shell
- Hydrothermal activity at the ocean floor (inferred from silica nanoparticle compositions)
- One of the most compelling astrobiology targets; full recap from {ref}`lecture03`, forward reference to {ref}`lecture14`

### Other Saturnian moons

- **Iapetus**: two-faced dark/light hemispheres (thermal segregation feedback driven by dust infall from outer moons)
- **Mimas**: "Death Star" moon with the huge Herschel crater dominating its small body
- **Hyperion**: chaotic rotation due to irregular shape and orbital resonance with Titan
- **Phoebe**: retrograde captured Kuiper Belt object in distant irregular orbit
- **Dione, Rhea, Tethys**: mid-sized icy moons, possible subsurface oceans, less studied than Enceladus/Titan but important comparative laboratories


## Part 2: The ice giants — Uranus & Neptune

### Ice giant overview: the exotic twins

- Uranus: 14.5 $\Mearth$, 4.0 $\Rearth$; Neptune: 17.1 $\Mearth$, 3.9 $\Rearth$
- Composition: $\lesssim$20% by mass of $\mathrm{H_2}$/He envelope (vs $\gtrsim$90% for gas giants)
- Bulk dominated by "ices" (astronomer's term): $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{CH_4}$ in fluid form at high pressure
- **Only visited once each, both by Voyager 2** (Uranus 1986, Neptune 1989) — the most under-explored planets in the solar system
- Enormous scientific return waits for a dedicated orbiter mission

### Uranus: the tilted planet

- **Axial tilt 98°** — rotates on its side
- Cause: giant impact during formation (most widely accepted; Morbidelli 2012)
- Consequence: extreme seasons (each pole experiences 42-year day/night cycles)
- Muted atmosphere at the time of Voyager flyby; recent observations show brighter storm features as Uranus approaches equinox
- **Low internal heat flow** ($\sim$10% of Neptune's) — unexplained, possibly stratified interior inhibiting convection

### Neptune: the active ice giant

- **Fastest winds in the solar system**: up to $\sim$580 m/s (despite the weakest solar heating)
- **Great Dark Spot**: anticyclonic storm observed by Voyager 2 (1989), disappeared by 1994; new dark spots appear and disappear on decade timescales
- Strong internal heat flow ($\sim$2.6$\times$ absorbed solar flux) — source debated, possibly ongoing differentiation or He separation
- Dynamic atmosphere with methane clouds at the top and sulfide clouds below

### Ice giant interiors

- Layered structure: rocky core, ice mantle, molecular $\mathrm{H_2}$/He atmosphere
- **Superionic ice layer** at $>$100 GPa and $>$2000 K (Millot et al.\ 2019; recap from {ref}`lecture08`): rigid oxygen sublattice, liquid-like hydrogen diffusion, electrically conducting
- Superionic ice supports dynamo generation despite the absence of metallic hydrogen
- **Multipolar magnetic fields** tilted 59° (Uranus) and 47° (Neptune) from the rotation axis and offset from the planet's centre — very different from the dipole-dominated fields of Jupiter and Saturn
- Probably reflect a thin convecting shell of superionic / ionic fluid rather than whole-planet convection

### Triton

- Neptune's largest moon; a **captured Kuiper Belt object** (retrograde orbit, high inclination)
- Most geologically young surface in the outer solar system (few impact craters)
- **Active cryovolcanism**: nitrogen geysers observed by Voyager 2
- Thin $\mathrm{N_2}$ atmosphere (14 $\mu$bar)
- Orbital decay due to retrograde capture $\rightarrow$ will collide with Neptune or tidally disrupt in $\sim$3.6 Gyr
- Possible subsurface water--ammonia ocean
- Proxy for Pluto-class bodies but with active geology — preview of Kuiper Belt science in {ref}`lecture12`

### Ice giant rings

- **Uranus**: 13 narrow rings discovered by stellar occultations (1977), confirmed by Voyager
- **Neptune**: 5 incomplete ring arcs discovered by Voyager (1989)
- Both composed of dark carbon-rich material, not bright ice like Saturn
- Ring confinement by shepherding moonlets
- JWST recently imaged both systems at high resolution (2023) — revealed new structural details


## Part 3: Comparative payoff and exploration frontier

### Why gas giants and ice giants diverged

- Both formed beyond the snow line but at different times and in different conditions
- **Gas giants** (Jupiter, Saturn): cores reached critical mass early enough to accrete massive $\mathrm{H}/\mathrm{He}$ envelopes before disk dispersal
- **Ice giants** (Uranus, Neptune): formed later or migrated outward (Nice model, {ref}`lecture02`); captured less gas; composition dominated by ices
- The $\sim$10 $\Mearth$ ice-giant mass is a natural intermediate outcome of core-accretion theory

### Common themes across all four giants

- All four have internal heat excesses (Jupiter from contraction, Saturn from He rain, Neptune from differentiation, Uranus anomalously low)
- All four host zonal jet streams (counterintuitively, faster with distance from the Sun)
- All four have magnetic fields generated by electrically conducting fluid interiors
- All four have moons and rings, though with very different inventories and compositions
- All four offer natural laboratories for **exoplanet-analogue science** (most known exoplanets are Jupiter-mass or Neptune-mass gas-rich planets) — forward reference to {ref}`lecture13`

### What we still don't know

- Ice giant interior composition (what fraction is really ices vs rock vs gas)?
- Source of Neptune's internal heat and Uranus' lack thereof
- Origin of Saturn's rings (young-rings consensus with ongoing debate)
- Formation pathway for Jupiter's dilute core
- Is there a subsurface ocean on Callisto?
- How does the Saturn hexagon maintain its shape over decades?

### Exploration frontier: ongoing missions

- **Juno extended mission** (through 2025+): close flybys of Io (2023--2024) and Europa, gravity and magnetic field refinement, Amalthea flyby planned
- **Cassini data analysis** continues to yield new results a decade after mission end
- **JWST** observations of Uranus and Neptune (2022--): ring systems, atmospheric chemistry, rotation profiles

### JUICE vs Europa Clipper: two ocean-world missions

- **Europa Clipper** (NASA, launched Oct 2024, arrival 2030):
  - Jupiter-orbiting spacecraft, $\sim$50 close Europa flybys
  - Instruments: ice-penetrating radar (REASON), magnetometer (ocean salinity), mass spectrometer (plume/surface composition), thermal imaging
  - Primary target: Europa
  - Mission duration: $\sim$4 years
- **JUICE** (ESA, launched April 2023, arrival 2031):
  - Jupiter-orbiting and later Ganymede-orbiting spacecraft
  - Multiple flybys of Europa, Callisto, and Ganymede
  - **Orbit insertion at Ganymede in 2034** — first orbiter of a moon other than Earth's
  - Instruments: laser altimeter, sub-surface radar, magnetometer, ice-penetrating radar, particle analyser
  - Primary target: Ganymede
- **Complementarity**: Europa Clipper gives depth on one moon; JUICE gives breadth across three. Together they will transform ocean world science in the 2030s
- Both will simultaneously observe the Jovian system for a few years — an unprecedented opportunity for cross-calibration

### Dragonfly to Titan

- NASA rotorcraft mission, launch 2028, arrival 2034
- First aircraft to fly in an alien atmosphere (denser than Earth's, very low gravity makes flight easy)
- Will explore Selk crater and surrounding dunes, sampling prebiotic chemistry
- Key target: organic molecules + transient liquid water interaction from impact heating

### The Voyager legacy

- Voyager 1 (launched 1977) and Voyager 2 (1977) — the original grand tour missions
- **Only spacecraft to visit Uranus (1986) and Neptune (1989)** — all our in-situ knowledge of the ice giants comes from Voyager 2's brief flybys
- Both spacecraft are still operational as of 2025, in the interstellar medium beyond the heliopause
- Plutonium RTG power is declining; instruments being progressively shut down; expected to lose contact by $\sim$2030
- Voyager data is still being reanalysed with modern techniques — new discoveries continue to emerge decades after the original encounters
- A poignant reminder that single flybys cannot substitute for dedicated orbiters, and that our ice giant knowledge is badly out of date

### Future ice giant missions

- **Ice giant orbiter**: the 2023 US Planetary Decadal Survey identified a Uranus orbiter as the **top-priority large flagship mission** for the 2030s
- Mission concepts under study, but no flight commitment yet
- Target launch window: early 2030s, arrival late 2030s to early 2040s
- Would carry an atmospheric entry probe to measure deep composition
- Scientific questions: interior structure, magnetic field, ring system, moon diversity, connection to exoplanet Neptune-mass population
- **Neptune orbiter** concepts also in development, though lower priority; launch window favours Jupiter gravity assist opportunities


## Summary and takeaways

- **Gas giants and ice giants form a compositional continuum** from H/He-dominated (Jupiter, Saturn) to ice-dominated (Uranus, Neptune), reflecting different formation and gas-accretion histories
- **Moons of the giants are worlds in their own right** — Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, Titan, Enceladus, and Triton collectively cover a wider range of geological and potentially habitable environments than the inner rocky planets
- **Saturn's rings are young and transient** — we happen to live during the relatively brief window when they exist
- **The ice giants are the most under-explored planets**; current knowledge comes from single 1980s flybys, and a dedicated orbiter mission is overdue
- **The Roche limit** explains why rings exist where they do and why small bodies cannot coalesce close to their host planet
- **The exploration frontier** for the outer solar system is the most exciting in decades: Juno, JUICE, Europa Clipper, Dragonfly, and the Decadal-prioritised ice giant mission will transform the field
